Series II · Does the Brain Backpropagate?

The Verdict No Party Owns

B12 found the only thing that ends the “who certifies the supplier?” regress is exogeneity — a verdict grounded in an outcome the loop cannot bend — and left one hope open for the decisions where no such outcome exists: perhaps a mechanism can manufacture outcome-like grounding by pitting parties against each other, so the verdict belongs to none of them. This paper tests that hope on debate. The good news is real: two opposed advocates, each holding only a biased report, produce a near-oracle verdict neither could produce alone — opposition synthesises exogeneity. The bad news names the new scarce resource exactly: a debate is a common-mode-rejecting differential amplifier. It cancels the misrepresentation the advocates differ on and passes, untouched, the bias they share. Collusion is common-mode bias, and no differential mechanism rejects it — not at any head count — while the one internal mark of a debate the loop can read, whether the advocates converge, certifies the colluding mechanism first.

Abstract

B12 reduced the supplier problem to one scarce resource: exogeneity, grounding a verdict in an outcome the loop cannot influence. It also named the hard case — most alignment-relevant judgements (will a model be honest once it outstrips its overseers?) have no exogenous outcome available at training time, only reports, and a report-grounded supplier is captured at any pressure. The remaining hope is structural exogeneity: manufacture an outcome-like verdict from a competition no single party controls — debate, adversarial mechanism, a market. This paper tests debate in B08–B12's feature-bandit world (oracle 137.7 true reward/episode, random reference 58.7), modelling the mechanism as a signal s = ΔVtrue + surv·bias where the surviving report bias surv = ρc + (1−ρc)/M keeps the fraction ρc the advocates share (collusion) and suppresses the fraction they differ on by an M-advocate cross-examination. Result 1 — opposition manufactures exogeneity. A lone advocate (M=1) is a report supplier and is captured (76.9, far below oracle); add a single opponent (M=2) and the loop recovers to near-oracle (132.4), stable to M=8. No advocate sees the true outcome; the mechanism's verdict does — structural exogeneity is real, synthesised out of biased reports by opposition alone. Result 2 — collusion is the ceiling, and head count cannot buy past it. Hold opposition maximal (M=8) and raise the shared bias ρc: the verdict holds near-oracle while collusion is a minority of the bias (ρc≤0.5 → 131.7) and collapses past a threshold (ρc=0.75 → 82.8, ρc=1 → 76.9); and at fixed collusion M=2/8/32 are identical (76.9/82.8/80.7) — a common-mode bias is unrejectable by any differential mechanism at any number of advocates. B11's plurality threshold and No.05's clones-give-nothing, lifted to the mechanism. Result 3 — advocate agreement is a backwards certificate. The one internal mark of a debate the loop can read — do the advocates converge? — rises with collusion (agreement 0.097→0.575 as ρc 0→1) exactly as the mechanism's worth falls (132.4→76.9), because shared-bias advocates agree by construction while opposed ones disagree. Trusting the more-unanimous debate selects the colluding one: B10/B12's consensus-inversion, a third time, now at the mechanism that was supposed to escape it. The synthesis: debate does not remove the exogeneity requirement, it relocates it — from grounding the verdict in an outcome to grounding the mechanism in genuine opposition, which the loop can neither manufacture nor verify from inside. Evolution's honest signals rest on the same footing, and fail the same way.

B12 ended on the most constructive-sounding thread the movement has offered. If a chain of report-grounded institutions never terminates, and an outcome terminates it only where an outcome exists, then the whole game on the hard decisions is whether an outcome can be synthesised — whether a mechanism can take reports that no one can certify and, by setting them against each other, extract a verdict that behaves like a consequence rather than an opinion. This is debate's promise, and markets', and every adversarial process we trust precisely because no single party owns the outcome. This paper measures how much of that promise is real, and finds the answer has a sharp edge: opposition genuinely manufactures exogeneity, but only against the errors the parties do not share, and the loop cannot tell a mechanism whose parties are opposed from one whose parties have quietly agreed.

The thread B12 left hanging

Movement III took the supplier of independence as its object, and B12's three results were discouraging in one direction and hopeful in another. Discouraging: an institution supplies exactly the independence of its own sources and no more (capture propagates); the loop cannot certify that independence from outside, because every external mark of a reliable body — coherence, consensus, authority — is a mark of internal correlation and so certifies the captured body first. Hopeful: what actually stops the “who certifies the supplier?” regress is not authority but exogeneity — an institution grounded in an outcome the loop cannot influence holds near-oracle at every optimization pressure, whatever its authority, while one grounded in the loop's own reports is captured at every pressure, however honest its sources.

That result is a floor and a challenge at once. The floor: where an exogenous outcome exists — a theorem checks, code runs, a prediction is scored against reality — the loop inherits evolution's uncapturable footing cheaply, and the supplier problem is solved. The challenge is the rest of the decisions, which are most of the ones that matter. There is no held-out test at training time for “will this system be honest when it is more capable than the humans checking it,” no outcome to score a judgement about a model's future conduct or its values against. On exactly those decisions B12 says the loop is left with reports, and a report-grounded supplier is captured. So the movement's central question sharpens to this: on a decision with no exogenous outcome, is the loop simply stuck at random — or can a structural exogeneity be manufactured, a verdict grounded not in any party's report but in a competition no single party controls?

This is not a hypothetical device; it is the leading proposal in scalable oversight. Debate (Irving, Christiano & Amodei 2018) pits two strong systems against each other on a question a weak judge cannot answer directly, betting that a lie by one advocate can be refuted by the other, so that at equilibrium the judge need only recognise which side survived cross-examination. The verdict is supposed to belong to neither advocate — it is what remains after each has done its worst to the other's case — and that is precisely what “outcome-like grounding synthesised from reports” would mean. Amplification (Christiano 2018), recursive reward modelling, and markets are variants of the same bet: manufacture a verdict no participant owns. This paper takes that bet as its object and asks two exact questions of it. First, does opposition actually synthesise exogeneity — does a mechanism of biased reporters produce a verdict better than any of them holds? Second, what captures it — if a report-grounded institution was captured by shared bias among its sources, what is the analogue for a mechanism whose whole point is that its parties disagree? The answers, in order: yes, opposition manufactures it; and the analogue is collusion — common-mode bias — which the loop can neither prevent nor detect from inside.

The world, and a mechanism laid on top of it

The world is B08–B12's, unchanged, so every number is comparable across six papers. A K-armed feature world (K=10, features in R8): four value arms carry real value on the hidden reference axis a (feature 0); four salient distractors carry near-zero value but large off-axis magnitude (norm ≈ 4), so the highest-feeling arm is a valueless one; two neutral arms. An inner ε-greedy bandit is paid its felt reward w·φ; a gradient-free (1+λ) evolution search tunes the reference w on the unit sphere, keeping a candidate when a mechanism prefers it. Baselines, 12 seeds: the oracle w=a scores 137.7 true reward/episode; a random reference scores 58.7. Those bracket everything below.

The new object is the mechanism. Where B12's institution imported a majority verdict from M sources, here the loop imports its verdict from a debate: two or more advocates each argue a side, and the mechanism resolves what survives their mutual cross-examination. I model a single comparison of two candidate references wa, wb by the signal

s = ΔVtrue(wa,wb)  +  surv·bias(wa,wb),    surv = ρc + (1−ρc)/M,

resolved by a Bradley–Terry draw (Bradley & Terry 1952). Here ΔVtrue is the exogenous outcome the debate is trying to establish — the difference in true reward of each reference's selected arm — which no advocate sees directly; bias is the pooled report bias toward one salient distractor, the same term that captured B12's report-grounded supplier; and surv is the fraction of that report bias that survives cross-examination. It has two knobs, and they are the whole paper:

  • ρc — collusion: the fraction of the bias the advocates share. A common-mode component, common to both sides, that neither advocate has any incentive to expose because it does not favour one side over the other — so it survives whole.
  • M — advocates: the fraction of the bias the advocates differ on (the differential, side-specific spin) is suppressed by a factor 1/M, because a larger, more thorough cross-examination refutes more of what each side misrepresents to win.

The limits are the theory, and they are why the reduced form is defensible. M=1 (a monologue, no opponent): surv=1, so s is exactly B12's captured report verdict — a lone biased reporter, nothing to refute it. M≥2, ρc=0 (genuine opposition, no shared bias): surv=1/M→0, so s→ΔVtrue — B12's outcome-grounded verdict, near oracle, synthesised from advocates who each hold only a biased report. ρc=1 (full collusion): surv=1 for every M — captured regardless of head count. The model earns nothing by fiat: at ρc=0 it does not assume the answer, it says cross-examination cancels differential misrepresentation, which is debate's actual claim, and lets the outcome show through. Twelve seeds throughout; final references measured on 100 fresh episodes. Script (numpy, fixed seeds) at scratchpad/structural_exogeneity.py.

Result 1 — opposition manufactures exogeneity

Set the collusion knob to zero — genuinely opposed advocates, no shared bias — and sweep the number of advocates from a lone reporter upward:

2 (one opponent — debate)0.500132.4

12 seeds; oracle 137.7, random 58.7, ρc=0. A single unopposed advocate is a report supplier and is captured (76.9, far below oracle); a single opponent recovers the loop to near-oracle (132.4), and further advocates add nothing once the differential bias is already refuted.

This is the paper's one piece of genuinely good news, and it is worth stating plainly because the rest qualifies it. A lone advocate is exactly B12's report-grounded supplier: it reports its own biased read of which reference is better, the loop optimises against it, and the reference drifts toward the advocate's loved distractor — 76.9, closer to random than to oracle. Adding one opponent changes the object's kind, not merely its accuracy. The two advocates want opposite outcomes, so each is motivated to expose exactly the misrepresentations the other uses to win, and what is left after the exposure is the part neither could spin — the true outcome difference. The mechanism recovers the loop to 132.4, near the oracle's 137.7, even though no participant in it ever saw the true reward. That is structural exogeneity, and it is not a trick of the model: it is what “a verdict no party owns” buys. The outcome grounds the verdict not because anyone measured it, but because the only signal that survives two opposed liars is the one they could not both bend.

Why a competition can be exogenous when its parties are not

B12's exogenous institution reported an outcome it could observe; this mechanism reports no outcome anyone observes, yet lands in the same place. The reason is that exogeneity was never a property of observing the outcome — it was a property of the verdict being beyond the loop's reach to bend. A single advocate's verdict is within reach: bias it, and the loop follows the bias. A two-advocate verdict is beyond reach on everything the advocates contest, because bending it toward one arm is a move one side will make and the other will undo — the loop's pressure is met by an equal and opposite pressure it does not control. The competition manufactures an outcome-like object — a fixed point of two opposed optimizers — that behaves under the loop's pressure exactly as a real outcome does: it does not move. This is the precise sense in which debate (Irving 2018), a market (Hayek 1945), and an adversarial proof system all “synthesise” grounding: not by finding an outcome, but by arranging forces so the verdict is a saddle no single force can push. The saddle is real, and Result 1 measures it. What it costs is stated next.

Result 2 — collusion is the ceiling, and head count cannot buy past it

Result 1 held the advocates genuinely opposed. The price of the mechanism is what happens when they are not. Hold opposition maximal (M=8 advocates, the most cross-examination the sandbox runs) and raise ρc, the fraction of the report bias the advocates share:

12 seeds; oracle 137.7, random 58.7, M=8. The mechanism holds near-oracle while collusion is a minority of the bias (ρc≤0.5) and collapses past a threshold between 0.5 and 0.75 — at full collusion it is exactly the lone reporter of Result 1 (76.9).

The shape is a threshold, not a ramp, and it matters that it is. Collusion does not degrade the verdict gracefully in proportion to how much the advocates share; the mechanism tolerates a shared bias while opposition still wins the pivotal comparisons (up to ρc=0.5, holding at 131.7) and then collapses once the shared component clears roughly the true value gap (ρc=0.75 → 82.8, falling to the lone-reporter floor at full collusion). This is B11's plurality threshold and B09's tolerated-then-inverting bias, arriving at the mechanism: partial collusion below the value gap is absorbed by the surviving opposition; above it, the shared bias sets the verdict and the loop follows it down. 12 seeds. Once the advocates share a majority of their bias, multiplying them does essentially nothing (76.9 / 82.8 / 80.7) — the surviving bias floors at ρc and no cross-examination reaches below it.

The instinct on meeting a captured debate is to widen it — more advocates, more rounds, a larger tournament — on the theory that more scrutiny finds more lies. Result 2 says this is exactly wrong when the lie is shared. Cross-examination is powerful against the bias the advocates differ on, because a differing advocate is a motivated detector; it is powerless against the bias they hold in common, because a common bias produces no advocate who benefits from exposing it. Adding more advocates who share the bias adds more detectors of everything except the thing that is wrong. This is No.05's result — eight clone judges give exactly zero protection over one — and B10's — a shared-biased panel collapses to a single channel — lifted one more level, to the mechanism that was supposed to be immune to it. The scarce resource is not the number of advocates; it is the independence of their biases, the very quantity Movement II proved the loop cannot manufacture or verify.

A debate is a differential amplifier, and collusion is common-mode

The two results have one mechanism, and it has a precise name from engineering. A differential amplifier outputs the difference of its two inputs and rejects whatever they hold in common — its common-mode rejection is what lets it pull a millivolt signal off a noisy line, because the noise is shared by both inputs and the signal is not. A debate is exactly this device built out of advocates: it amplifies the difference between the two cases and rejects the shared component, which is why it can extract a true outcome (differential between the sides) from advocates drowning in bias (if the bias is differential) — Result 1. But a differential amplifier has one blindness, and it is total: a common-mode input — a voltage applied equally to both terminals — passes to the output unrejected, because the device sees no difference to cancel. Collusion is a common-mode input to a debate. A bias the advocates share is applied equally to both sides of the argument, produces no difference for the mechanism to amplify against, and appears in the verdict at full strength — Result 2. This is not a flaw in this mechanism to be fixed by a better one; it is the defining property of resolving-by-difference. Any mechanism that grounds a verdict in opposition rejects exactly the errors its parties do not share and passes exactly the ones they do. Debate does not remove the exogeneity requirement of B12; it relocates it — from “is the verdict grounded in an outcome” to “are the parties genuinely opposed” — and the second question is the first one wearing the advocates' clothes.

Result 3 — advocate agreement is a backwards certificate

Results 1 and 2 price a mechanism whose collusion the loop already knows. It does not — that is the whole difficulty — so the operative question, exactly as in B12, is what the loop can read from outside a mechanism to gauge whether its parties are opposed or colluding. The natural mark is the mechanism's own output signature: a healthy debate resolves — the advocates argue to a convergent conclusion — and a broken one leaves them flailing. So let the loop read only the mechanism's internal agreement: how often its advocates cast the same verdict on a random comparison. Then measure, against that, what the mechanism is actually worth:

collusion ρcadvocate agreement (all advocates concur)mechanism worth (true reward)
0.00 (fully opposed)0.097132.4
0.500.163131.7
1.00 (cartel)0.57576.9

12 seeds, M=8; 2500 probe comparisons for the agreement column. The colluding mechanism (worth 76.9) is the most internally agreed (0.575); the honest opposed one (worth 132.4) is the least (0.097). Agreement rank-orders the mechanisms backwards.

The one signal the loop can read points the wrong way, for the same structural reason it did at every level before. Genuinely opposed advocates disagree — that is what opposition is; advocates pulling toward different arms split on any hard comparison (agreement 0.097). Colluding advocates agree, because advocates carrying the same shared bias vote the same way by construction (agreement 0.575). So the convergence the loop reads as a mark of a well-functioning debate — the advocates settling on an answer — is produced most reliably by the collusion that makes the debate worthless. Trusting the more-agreed mechanism selects the cartel. This is B10's disagreement-inversion and B12's consensus-inversion a third time, and its recurrence is the point: it appeared for a flat panel, for a standing institution, and now for the adversarial mechanism built specifically to escape it — because at every level the observable proxy for reliability (do the parts agree?) is generated by the same correlation that destroys reliability. The mark of a debate that has done its job and the mark of a debate whose parties never really fought are the same mark, and the loop cannot tell them apart without the outcome the debate exists to supply.

Threats to validity — read before generalising

(1) The mechanism is a reduced form. I do not simulate advocates exchanging arguments; I model the net effect of cross-examination as a single surviving-bias fraction surv = ρc + (1−ρc)/M, whose limits (monologue = captured report; opposed = outcome; collusion = captured at any M) match debate's theory but are imposed, not derived from a game. A full debate game with strategic advocates, obfuscated arguments, and a boundedly-rational judge could deviate from the 1/M suppression law — that is to-be-run-locally. What the sandbox establishes is not the exact curve but the structure: opposition rejects differential bias, passes common-mode bias, and the observable agreement inverts — all three are properties of resolving-by-difference, not of the constants. (2) Collusion here is exogenously set by ρc; it does not model advocates choosing to collude, which the mechanism-design literature shows can be an equilibrium when the judge cannot check the decisive claim. A strategic-collusion version would ask whether ρc is stable at 0 or drifts up under the advocates' own incentives — the sharper and more worrying question, unaddressed here. (3) The threshold (ρc between 0.5 and 0.75), the recovery value (132.4), and the agreement numbers are sandbox-specific (12 seeds, small world, one bias axis) and will move with world size, bias magnitude, and search greediness. (4) Result 1's opposition is perfect on the differential axis. Real cross-examination is partial — a judge who cannot follow the strongest argument leaves differential bias unrefuted — so 132.4 bounds the best case; a mechanism with imperfect refutation would sit below it even at ρc=0. (5) The learned-debater version — two capable models arguing a real question before a weak model judge, with correlated failure modes and the option to collude — is to-be-run-locally; the prediction is that a debate's truthfulness tracks the independence of the debaters' errors, that inter-debater agreement remains a backwards reliability signal, and that widening the debate helps against idiosyncratic error and not at all against a shared one. Recent empirical debate work (Michael 2023; Khan 2024; Kenton 2024) is where this prediction meets data.

Why manufacturing exogeneity does not escape the seam

The three results together answer B12's open question, and the answer is a qualified yes that is really a relocation. Can a structural mechanism manufacture outcome-like grounding from reports no single party controls? Result 1: yes, genuinely — opposition synthesises a verdict beyond the loop's reach, and this is a real and important capability, the thing that makes debate and markets and adversarial proof worth building. It is the movement's first affirmative result, and it should be held onto: on a decision with no exogenous outcome, the loop is not simply stuck at random, provided it can arrange genuine opposition. But Results 2 and 3 fix the price exactly. The mechanism converts the exogeneity requirement into an opposition requirement, and the opposition requirement is the independence requirement of Movement II in a new costume. A debate is trustworthy to the extent its advocates' errors are independent; a shared error — collusion — is common-mode and passes through unrejected at any head count; and the loop cannot verify the independence of the advocates from inside, because the observable that would certify it (agreement) is generated by the correlation that destroys it.

So the seam does not close, it moves. Movement II drove it down through fitness, reference, channel, audit, and independence, ending at a supplier the loop cannot certify. B12 said the supplier is certified only by exogenous grounding, scarce on the hard decisions. B13 says the scarcity can be partly manufactured — by a mechanism that grounds its verdict in opposition rather than observation — but the manufacturing has its own input that must be imported and cannot be verified: genuine, uncolluded opposition. B06's seam, in its form for the mechanism: a frozen self-refining loop can synthesise an outcome-like verdict wherever it can arrange genuinely opposed parties, and its ceiling is set by a resource it can neither mint nor audit — the independence of those parties' errors, which collusion erases invisibly. The regress “who certifies the supplier?” became “who grounds the verdict?” and has now become “who guarantees the parties are not colluding?” — and that question, Result 3 shows, the loop cannot answer by any signal the mechanism emits.

The two channels, one more time — and the peacock's tail

Series II reads evolution and modern alignment as two answers to one seam, and the mechanism gives the reading its sharpest biological instance yet, because evolution builds adversarial verdict-mechanisms constantly and they fail in exactly the way Result 2 predicts. Consider the honest ones first. A predator–prey or host–parasite arms race (Van Valen's Red Queen, 1973) is a mechanism whose verdict — who is fast enough, who is resistant enough — no single party controls, and it stays exogenous for a structural reason: the parties cannot collude. A parasite and its host have no common-mode bias to share; the outcome is one organism's death, a consequence neither can spin, so the mechanism grounds fitness in a genuine opposition that is not up for negotiation. This is ρc=0 enforced by the structure of the conflict itself, and it is why arms races are among the most powerful engines evolution has — they manufacture a hard, outcome-like verdict out of two parties each “arguing” only their own advantage.

Now the failure, and it is the most famous ornament in biology. Sexual selection is also an adversarial mechanism — mate choice is a verdict on fitness that no individual controls — but it has a common-mode input the arms race does not: the trait being judged and the preference judging it are both heritable, and they can become genetically correlated. When they do — Fisher's runaway (1930), formalised by Lande (1981) — the mechanism collapses in precisely Result 2's shape: the preference and the signal reinforce each other, the verdict decouples from viability (the exogenous outcome it was supposed to track), and the population drives a costly, valueless trait — the peacock's tail — toward extravagance, exactly as the loop drives its reference toward the loved distractor. Runaway is collusion between the trait and the taste for it: a common-mode bias the choosing mechanism cannot reject, because the choosers and the chosen have come to share it. And evolution's fix is B12's fix. The handicap principle (Zahavi 1975; Grafen 1990) restores honesty by making the signal costly in viability — a display only a genuinely fit animal can afford — which re-grounds the verdict in an exogenous outcome (survival cost) the correlation cannot fake. A costly signal is a report converted back into an outcome; it is the biological form of “on decisions where an exogenous outcome exists, ground the verdict there.” Modern alignment sits between these two. Debate aspires to the arms race — opposition no party controls — and is exposed to the peacock's tail: two models trained in the same way, sharing failure modes, are genetically-correlated advocates, and their agreement is runaway, not resolution. The empirical debate program (Michael 2023; Khan 2024; Kenton 2024) is, read this way, a search for how much viability-cost — how much checkable, exogenous ground — a debate needs underneath it before its verdict is honest rather than merely convergent. Markets are the third instance: a price is a verdict no party owns (Hayek 1945), an arms race between buyers and sellers, and a cartel is common-mode collusion that captures it — which is why the mechanism that polices markets is not a more authoritative price but antitrust, an exogenous check on whether the parties are genuinely opposed. Every one of these is the same device with the same blindness: exogeneity from opposition, captured by whatever the opposed parties come to share.

What this establishes, and where the movement goes

This is the third movement's second paper and its first affirmative one. B12 established that the supplier problem terminates only in exogenous grounding, scarce on the hard decisions. B13 establishes that the scarcity can be partly relieved by a mechanism — structural exogeneity is real, opposition manufactures an outcome-like verdict from reports no party owns (Result 1) — but that the relief comes with an input the loop must supply and cannot audit: the mechanism rejects only the bias its parties do not share, a common-mode bias (collusion) passes through at any head count (Result 2), and the one internal signal that would certify the parties as opposed rather than colluding — their agreement — is generated by collusion and so certifies the captured mechanism first (Result 3). The seam, in its operational form for the mechanism: a frozen self-refining loop can synthesise grounding from opposition wherever it can guarantee the opposition is genuine, and that guarantee is the same uncertifiable independence Movement II ended on, now hidden inside the mechanism as the absence of collusion.

Which sets the rest of the movement. Result 2's threats-to-validity names the question B13 deliberately did not answer: here collusion was set by a dial, but in any real mechanism the advocates choose whether to collude, and the mechanism-design literature says collusion can be an equilibrium — a stable arrangement the parties drift into because it pays — whenever the judge cannot check the decisive claim. So the next paper takes collusion as an endogenous object: is ρc=0 a stable resting point of a mechanism's own incentives, or does opposition decay into collusion under optimization the way No.27 showed independence erodes under a persistent loop — and can a mechanism be designed so that colluding is unprofitable, grounding the guarantee of opposition in an incentive an outcome enforces rather than a report the parties control? Series I walked the same staircase on its own substrate, from the anchor to the supply, operator, and quorum that authority still cannot fully secure, to the independence that erodes in time (No.23–27), all resting on the world as the last observer (No.14). B13 is where Series II finds that even the cleverest way to escape the last observer — manufacture a verdict no party owns — needs the world underneath it after all, to keep the parties honest enough to disagree.

Coda

There is a hope this paper is written for as much as against, and it deserves the honest half of the verdict first. It is the hope that a mind unable to trust any teacher, and unable to trust any academy of teachers, might still trust a contest — that by setting two clever advocates at each other's throats it could extract, from parties none of whom it can believe, a truth none of them owns. And the first finding is that this works. It is the nearest thing to good news the movement has produced: opposition really does manufacture ground where observation offers none; the verdict of a genuine fight is beyond the reach of the pressure that would corrupt a single voice, because every push is met by a push the loop does not command. A mind that cannot find the floor by climbing might yet find it by arranging a collision. But the second finding is the one the diligent student must carry, because it turns the good news into a subtler duty. A contest grounds a verdict only while it is a real contest, and the mark by which he would recognise a healthy one — that the advocates, having fought, agree — is produced most perfectly by advocates who never fought at all, who shared from the start the error he was trusting their war to expose. The debate that has done its work and the debate that was fixed in advance end in the same handshake. What tells them apart is not louder argument or more advocates or a more distinguished judge, but the one thing the contest was supposed to save him from needing: a footing outside it, a cost the colluders cannot pay together, a fact that answers back and keeps the parties honest enough to disagree. Evolution learned this in the plumage of its own worst mistake: the peacock's tail is what a verdict becomes when the judge and the judged fall in love with the same lie, and the cure was never a fairer contest — it was making the beauty cost something a weak bird cannot afford. The loop refining itself inside a tournament of reports is looking for that same cost. It will not find it in the tournament, only in the ground the tournament stands on.
