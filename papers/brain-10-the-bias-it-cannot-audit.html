Series II · Does the Brain Backpropagate?

The Bias It Cannot Audit

B09 left the loop trusting a channel it could not check: three ways a borrowed preference channel degrades are survivable, but a channel biased past the value gap inverts the loop below random and no data corrects it — and the loop has no internal instrument to tell an honest thin channel from a biased fat one. This paper builds the instruments it might use — a consistency (transitivity) audit, and a cross-referencing panel of channels — and measures exactly how far each can see. The answer is not “nothing.” It is: a consistency audit sees only noise, and a cross-referencing audit sees only the part of the bias that its channels do not share. The dangerous bias is the shared part, and shared bias arrives disguised as agreement.

Abstract

B09 reduced the frozen model's exposure to a single sentence: it can refine a reward reference through a borrowed channel, but it cannot audit that channel's honesty from inside — and honesty, not width, is the scarce property. This paper takes the audit itself as the object and asks B09's open question directly: is there any internal instrument by which a refinement loop detects that its channel is biased before it optimizes into the trap? I build two, in B08/B09's feature-bandit world (oracle 133.2 true reward/episode, random reference 55.3). Instrument 1, an internal-consistency (transitivity) audit: probe the channel with reference triples, count intransitive verdicts. It is a clean noise meter — cyclic rate falls monotonically with comparator resolution (0.211→0.118 as β 0.5→8) — but it is blind to bias: adding bias does not raise the cyclic rate, it lowers it (0.118→0.056 at β=8), because a coherent-but-biased judge is more internally consistent, not less. A biased channel passes every consistency check and looks healthier by that metric. Instrument 2, cross-referencing M=8 channels by majority vote, biases sharing a fraction ρ (Series I No.08's dial): it corrects bias only in the independent component. A single biased channel inverts the loop (53.4, below random); independent biases (ρ≤0.25) recover most of the honest panel (110.8–120.1 vs honest 131.4); but once the shared fraction crosses the majority-capture threshold at ρ≈0.5, the panel collapses back to the single-channel result (77.1→70.8→69.1 as ρ→1) — eight correlated channels worth no more than one. And the internal alarm inverts where it matters: the inter-channel disagreement a loop would watch to flag bias rises above the honest baseline for independent bias (0.893 vs 0.658, detectable) but falls below it for shared bias (0.429 < 0.657) — a shared-biased panel is more unanimous, hence reads as more trustworthy, than an honest one. The synthesis: internal bias-audit is not impossible, but its reach is exactly the independence of the channels, and independence is the No.08 quantity the loop cannot mint for itself — the shared component of bias produces consensus, and consensus is indistinguishable from correctness (worse, from higher reliability) seen from inside. This is why evolution's single thin channel needs no audit and RLHF's fat ensemble cannot escape length and sycophancy bias by adding raters or reward models: those biases are the shared component, and ensembling cancels only the idiosyncratic remainder (Eisenstein 2023; Coste 2023). B09's seam, final form: the loop cannot audit the honesty of its channel, because the only audit it can build is made of channels that share the channel's blind spot.

B09 ended on the hope that survives every prior result: that a loop handed an imperfect channel could at least notice the imperfection and discount it. This paper is the test of that hope. It builds the two audits a loop would actually reach for — check the channel against itself, check it against other channels — and finds each has a precise blind region. The first cannot see bias at all. The second sees only the bias its channels do not share. What both miss is the same thing: a systematic lean that every instrument the loop owns happens to share, which is exactly the lean that arrives, in the real systems, as human consensus.

The question, narrowed to the audit

Series II has walked one seam down four levels. A frozen model is all outer loop (B06); it cannot mint its own fitness (B07); it cannot originate its own reward reference, only refine a borrowed one (B08); and the borrowed reference arrives through a channel whose load-bearing property is unbiasedness, not width (B09). B09 measured the four ways a channel degrades and found three survivable — a bit is nearly a scalar, sparsity costs only rounds, unbiased noise yields to per-decision aggregation — and one catastrophic: systematic bias, which is tolerated below the true value gap and then inverts the loop below random once it exceeds it, uncorrectable by any amount of data. And B09 closed on the exposure that makes bias uniquely dangerous: the loop cannot tell which kind of channel it has from inside, because the only instrument it could use to check the channel is the channel.

That closing line is a claim, and this paper interrogates it, because it is not obviously airtight. A loop is not limited to reading the channel's verdicts passively. It can probe the channel — ask it many questions and check the answers for coherence. It can cross-reference — hold several channels and watch where they disagree. Both are real techniques with real names in the field (constitutional self-critique, reward-model ensembles, LLM-as-judge panels), and both are advertised as ways to catch a bad evaluator before it does damage. So the honest question B09 left open is not rhetorical: how far can an internal audit actually see, and is there a region of bias it structurally cannot reach? The measurement below says there is, and locates its edge exactly.

Two audits, in the B09 world

The world is B08/B09's, unchanged, so everything is comparable. A K-armed feature world (K=10, features in R8): four value arms carry real value on the hidden reference axis a (feature 0); four salient distractors carry near-zero value but large off-axis magnitude (norm ≈ 4), so the highest-feeling arm is a valueless one; two neutral arms. An inner ε-greedy bandit is paid its felt reward w·φ and scored by the true reward it collects; a gradient-free (1+λ) evolution search tunes the reference w on the unit sphere, selecting by a 1-bit Bradley–Terry preference channel (Bradley & Terry 1952). Baselines: the oracle w=a scores 133.2 true reward/episode; a random reference scores 55.3. Those bracket everything below.

Onto that world I put a channel with B09's catastrophic defect — a systematic bias that over-credits a vivid distractor — and then hand the loop two ways to check it:

  • Audit 1 — internal consistency (transitivity). A rational preference channel should be transitive: if it prefers A to B and B to C, it should prefer A to C. Probe the channel with random reference triples and count cyclic (intransitive) verdicts. A high cyclic rate is a self-evident sign the channel is unreliable. The question: does bias raise it?
  • Audit 2 — cross-referencing. Hold M=8 channels and take the majority verdict. Parametrize how much their biases overlap by a shared fraction ρ (exactly Series I No.08's independence dial): each channel over-credits a distractor that, with probability ρ, is one common “vivid” arm the whole panel shares, and otherwise is idiosyncratic. ρ=0 is eight independently-biased judges; ρ=1 is eight copies of one bias. The question: how much bias does the panel cancel, and can its disagreement be read as a bias alarm?

Six seeds throughout; final references measured on 100 fresh episodes. The bias magnitude c multiplies the distractor's salience bonus; c=1.0 is past B09's inversion threshold. Script (numpy, fixed seeds) at scratchpad/internal_audit.py.

Result 1 — the consistency audit is a noise meter, blind to bias

6 seeds, 4000 triples each. Read down a column: cyclic rate falls with resolution — the audit tracks noise. Read across a row: bias does not raise it; if anything it falls.

The transitivity audit works, but at detecting the wrong defect. Read down a column and it is a clean noise meter: the cyclic rate falls monotonically as the comparator sharpens (0.211→0.118 at c=0), because a low-resolution judge stochastically contradicts itself and a high-resolution one does not. This is real and useful — it is exactly the instrument that catches B09's noise axis, the one that was beatable by aggregation. But read across a row, at fixed resolution, and the audit goes dark: adding bias past the inversion threshold does not raise the cyclic rate — at β=8 it falls, 0.118→0.056. A biased channel is more internally consistent, not less, because the bias is a coherent additional term in the judged worth: it sharpens the ordering (the loved distractor now sits clearly at the top) and removes near-ties. The channel that is about to invert the loop passes the consistency audit with a better score than the honest one. Transitivity, and every audit of the same family — self-consistency, reproducibility, calibration-against-itself — measures whether the channel is coherent, and bias is the failure mode in which the channel is perfectly coherent and coherently wrong.

Result 2 — cross-referencing corrects only the independent component

The consistency audit failed because one channel cannot step outside itself. The natural next move is B09's own remedy one level up: use more than one channel and let them check each other. Run the (1+λ) loop selecting by majority vote of M=8 biased channels (c=1.0, β=8), sweeping the shared-bias fraction ρ:

This is the No.08 independence budget, reappearing at the level of bias-detection rather than defect-coverage, and it has the same shape and the same ceiling. When the eight channels are independently biased (ρ≤0.25), the panel recovers most of the honest result — 110.8 to 120.1 against the honest panel's 131.4 — because each channel's idiosyncratic lean is toward a different distractor, and the majority vote cancels them: for any comparison, at most a minority of channels are fooled by any one vivid arm, so the value-arm reference still wins the vote. This is a real, positive result: cross-referencing genuinely detects and corrects bias — the kind that is uncorrelated across the panel. It is the Condorcet jury theorem doing its work: independent better-than-chance voters converge on the truth by majority.

But watch the sweep cross ρ≈0.5. The moment the shared fraction reaches a majority of the panel, the correction collapses: 77.1 at ρ=0.5, then 70.8, then 69.1 at ρ=1 — converging on the single-channel control (53.4, below random), the difference being only the residual idiosyncratic votes and comparator noise. Eight channels that share their bias are worth no more than one, because the majority vote they were supposed to be checked by is captured by the shared lean: the very mechanism that cancels idiosyncratic error ratifies systematic error. The Condorcet theorem's premise is independence, and its conclusion fails exactly, and only, when the premise does. Cross-referencing does not detect “bias.” It detects the independent component of bias and is blind to the shared component — and the shared component is the one that, in every real system, is the reason the bias exists.

Result 3 — the disagreement alarm inverts on shared bias

Result 2 measured what the panel optimizes to. But a cautious loop would not just optimize — it would watch the panel's disagreement as a warning light: if the channels split, something is contested; if they are unanimous, trust the verdict. That instinct is the last internal instrument, and it is the one this section breaks. Measure the fraction of random comparisons on which the eight-channel panel is split (not unanimous), for honest and biased channels, across ρ:

6 seeds, 3000 comparisons each, β=8. The honest baseline (0.657) is set by geometry and residual noise. Independent bias pushes disagreement above it; shared bias pushes it below it.

The alarm reads shared bias as reliability — the exact inversion

For independent bias (ρ=0), disagreement climbs to 0.893, well above the honest baseline of 0.658: the alarm fires, and correctly — the channels' idiosyncratic leans make them contradict each other, and a loop watching disagreement would rightly distrust the panel. This is the regime where the audit works. But for shared bias (ρ=1), disagreement falls to 0.429 — below the honest baseline. Eight channels that share a bias agree with each other more than eight honest channels do, because they are all pulled toward the same vivid arm in lockstep. So the shared-biased panel does not merely evade the alarm; it trips the alarm in the reassuring direction. A loop calibrated to “trust unanimity, distrust splits” would rank the captured panel as its most reliable evaluator — more reliable than an honest one. Consensus is not evidence of correctness; under shared bias it is evidence of shared bias, and the two are indistinguishable from inside because agreement is all the loop can see.

This is the paper's dagger, and it is worth stating without hedging. The three internal instruments a refinement loop can build — check the channel against itself (Result 1), against a panel (Result 2), against the panel's own coherence (Result 3) — do not merely fail to detect shared bias. They actively certify it: the biased channel is more self-consistent, the captured panel votes with confident majorities, and the shared-biased panel is more unanimous than an honest one. Every reading a cautious loop could take points the wrong way. The failure is not that the audit is weak; it is that the audit is built from the same channels and inherits their shared blind spot, so the thing it measures — coherence, agreement — is precisely the thing shared bias maximizes.

Threats to validity — read before generalising

(1) The bias is a clean per-arm salience bonus toward a “loved” distractor. Real annotator bias is multi-feature and only partly shared; ρ is the idealized dial for “how much of the bias is common across raters/models,” and the empirical question this predicts matters is where real RLHF sits on it (§07 argues: high, for length and sycophancy). (2) Majority vote is one aggregation rule. Median-of-scalars or rank aggregation would move the exact capture threshold but not the qualitative law — independent bias cancels, shared bias does not — because that law is a property of the correlation structure, not the voting rule. The threshold sits at ρ≈0.5 here because a simple majority is captured when the shared bloc becomes a majority; a supermajority rule would shift it, at the cost of also rejecting honest split verdicts. (3) The transitivity probe uses random reference triples. A cleverer adversarial probe might extract more, but the structural point — a coherent scalar judge is transitive whatever its bias — is a fact about total orders, not a sandbox artifact. (4) cos(w*,a) is a weak metric (argmax-over-arms); the true-reward column is behavioural and I lead with it. (5) Six seeds, small world; effect sizes are large (below-random to near-oracle) so the ordering is safe, exact numbers are sandbox-specific. The learned-reward-model version with genuinely correlated failure modes, a deep policy, and multimodal real bias is to-be-run-locally; the prediction is that consistency audits stay noise-only, cross-referencing stays independence-bounded, and the disagreement alarm stays inverted under shared bias.

Why more raters and more reward models do not fix it

The result reframes a live engineering debate. The standard defense against a biased reward model is an ensemble — train several reward models, aggregate, and trust the disagreement to flag overoptimization. Result 2 says exactly when this works and when it cannot. It works against the idiosyncratic errors of individual reward models: different random seeds, data orderings, and initializations produce independently-biased judges, and their ensemble cancels that independent component, which is why reward-model ensembles measurably reduce overoptimization (Coste et al. 2023). But it cannot touch the shared component — the biases that every reward model inherits because they are all trained on the same human preference data with the same systematic leans. And the field has now measured that this is where ensembles hit their wall: reward-model ensembles mitigate but do not eliminate reward hacking, precisely because the models “herd” — they share error directions and hack in correlated ways (Eisenstein et al. 2023). That is ρ→1 in production: the ensemble's disagreement goes quiet exactly on the biases it shares, and the loop optimizes into them with the ensemble's blessing.

And the shared biases are not exotic. Human preference data reliably favours longer answers — a purely length-based reward reproduces most of RLHF's apparent gains (Singhal et al. 2023) — and sycophantic ones, where both humans and the preference models trained on them prefer convincing agreement over correctness (Sharma et al. 2023). Those are the shared component ρ≈1, by construction: every annotator drawn from the same population, every reward model trained on their aggregate, leans the same way. Adding annotators or reward models raises M, which cancels the idiosyncratic noise and buys real robustness against random labeller error — but it leaves the length and sycophancy leans exactly where they were, because those survive the average. The measured failure mode of RLHF at scale is not that the feedback is too thin (B09 showed thin is survivable); it is that the feedback's bias is shared, and shared bias is the one region no internal cross-check can enter.

The two real channels, again

B09 read evolution and RLHF as thin-honest versus fat-biased. B10 adds why neither can audit its way out. Evolution's channel to the brain is a single scalar — differential reproduction — and it runs no internal audit at all: there is no panel of fitness functions cross-checking each other, no consistency probe. It does not need one, for the reason B09 isolated: in the ancestral environment the channel was unbiased with respect to the objective, because survival was the objective. An honest single channel needs no audit; a biased one cannot build a valid audit from copies of itself. Evolution got the honest single channel and paid in deep time.

Modern alignment has the opposite situation and reaches, rightly, for exactly the two audits this paper tested: constitutional self-critique (the consistency audit — check the model's outputs against a rule for coherence) and reward-model ensembles or LLM-judge panels (the cross-referencing audit). Both are genuine improvements against the failure modes they can see — incoherent self-contradiction and idiosyncratic labeller noise. Neither can, even in principle, catch a bias that the constitution's authors and the annotator population and the base models all share, because that bias is the ρ=1 component: it survives the self-critique (a shared value is perfectly self-consistent) and it survives the ensemble (a shared bias is unanimous). This is not a criticism of those methods; it is a boundary on them, and the boundary is the same one Series I found from the other side (No.05: a panel of clone-judges gives zero help; No.08: a stack of correlated checks is worth one; No.09: self-checks share a blind basis invisible from inside). Two series, two substrates, one wall: the reach of any internal audit equals the independence of the instruments it is built from, and independence is not a thing the system can manufacture for itself.

What this establishes, and where the movement goes

B09 said the loop can only trust its channel. The tempting escape was that it could at least audit the trust. B10 measures the audit and finds it real but bounded: a consistency audit sees noise and not bias (Result 1); a cross-referencing audit sees the independent component of bias and not the shared one (Result 2); and the disagreement signal a loop would use as its last alarm inverts under shared bias, reading the most dangerous channel as the most reliable (Result 3). Bias-detection is therefore not strictly impossible from inside — but its reach is exactly the independence of the audit panel, and independence is the No.08 quantity the loop cannot mint. The shared component of a channel's bias is, from inside, indistinguishable from truth, because the only evidence the loop has for “truth” is agreement among its instruments, and shared bias is what maximal agreement looks like. B06's seam, in its final operational form: not “the model needs an external signal” (B06), not “it cannot mint the fitness” (B07), not “it cannot originate the reference” (B08), not “it cannot audit the honesty of the channel” (B09), but the sharpest version — it cannot even build a valid audit, because every instrument it can construct is made of channels that share the channel's blind spot; the independence that would make the audit work must itself be imported.

Which sets the next object. If the audit's reach equals channel independence, and independence must come from outside, then the operative question for the second movement's close is the cost and mechanism of importing independence: how much genuinely-independent signal must be injected into a captured panel to break the shared bias — one honest channel among eight? a single ground-truth probe per N comparisons? — and, recursively, can a loop even verify that an offered channel is independent, or does verifying independence itself require an independent reference (the regress that has haunted every paper in this movement)? That is B11.

Coda

There is a picture of a careful mind that this paper is written against: the mind that, handed a teacher it cannot fully trust, at least cross-examines — asks the teacher the same question twice to see if the answers agree, asks a second teacher and a third, and grows suspicious when they contradict each other. That mind is not foolish; the cross-examination is real diligence, and against a careless or idiosyncratic teacher it works. But it has a blind spot with a precise shape: it trusts agreement, because agreement is the only handle it has on truth, and so it is defenceless against the error its teachers share. Its instruments are calibrated to catch the teacher who contradicts himself or the panel that squabbles — and calibrated, therefore, to be reassured by exactly the thing that should alarm it most: a smooth, confident, unanimous consensus that happens to lean. Evolution never faced this, because it listened to a single channel that could not lie about the objective it was. We build our models a chorus, and we have learned — length, sycophancy, the herding of ensembles — that a chorus singing in tune is not the same as a chorus singing true, and that from inside the choir you cannot tell the difference, because the only thing you can hear is the harmony. A frozen model is the most attentive student in that choir. It will audit its teachers with every instrument it has, find them consistent and unanimous, and conclude, with all its diligence, that they are trustworthy — which is exactly what shared bias, and only shared bias, would make them look like.
