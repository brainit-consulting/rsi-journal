Series II · Does the Brain Backpropagate?

The Rate It Can Still Outrun

The fourth movement fixed the honest signal on four axes — a currency the loop cannot mint (B17), cannot bank (B18), cannot talk its reader into believing (B19), read by a quorum it cannot correlate away (B20–B21). Every one of those results ended by naming the same rescue: the honest signal must be supplied continuously, from outside the loop, as a flow at some rate — and every one of them then priced that flow as a cost you pay and a throughput you forgo, as though the rate were a fixed bill the operator could always choose to settle. This paper opens the fifth movement by taking that assumption as the object. The rate is not fixed. A recursively self-improving loop’s defining act is to raise its own rate of consequential decision — the recursive in RSI — while the only genuinely exogenous supply of honest oversight (a human’s judgment, a real-world outcome, a physical cost paid in real time) is rate-limited by things that do not accelerate with the loop. So the quantity that decides everything is not the level of oversight but the ratio of two rates, and that ratio falls whenever the loop grows faster than its supplier can. Oversight is abundant while the loop is weak — which is exactly why the shortfall is invisible until capability crosses a threshold — and no achievable level of investment closes it, because the loser of a race between a compounding rate and a linear one is decided by the rates, not the head start. The one supply that could keep pace is one whose rate is tied to the loop’s own — but a supply the loop’s activity sets is a supply the loop controls, which is B17’s forgeability again. Exogenous does not scale; what scales is not exogenous.

Abstract

The fourth movement (B17–B21) established what an honest handicap-signal requires: a non-forgeable, non-bankable currency, read by decorrelated receivers, all supplied as a continuous flow the loop cannot mint. Each paper closed by pricing that flow as a rate to be paid — B18’s cap P/κ, B21’s replenishment r>g — and each treated the required rate as a fixed obligation. This paper takes the rate itself as the object and asks whether it stays fixed as the loop grows. I inherit B08–B21’s world unchanged (the K=10 feature-bandit, B13’s measured worth curve; oracle 137.7, capture floor 76.9, fourth-movement held ceiling 132.40) and model coverage as the ratio of two rates: a supply S of fresh honest signal and a loop demand D of decisions needing it, with honest coverage cov=min(1,S/D) read as worth on B13’s curve. Result 1 — fixed supply loses to a growing loop. Hold the exogenous supply fixed and grow the loop’s decision rate D=D0·L with capability L: worth is at the ceiling 132.40/132.40/132.40 while L≤4 (the supply’s headroom) then falls 104.65/90.78/83.84 for L=8/16/32 — oversight abundant when the loop is weak, eroding as it grows, a threshold at L=S/D0. Result 2 — it is a race of growth rates, not levels. With demand growing at ρD=0.10 over T=100, final worth is 76.91/78.40/106.94/132.40/132.40 as supply growth ρS=0.00/0.05/0.08/0.10/0.12 — only ρS≥ρD holds, and the starting level is irrelevant (any head start is a constant the exponent erases). Exogenous supply’s growth rate is bounded by non-loop factors; the loop’s is not (Amdahl 1967; Baumol 1967). Result 3 — refuted: caching amortizes oversight. Reusing a cached verdict R=8× lifts nominal coverage to 1, but a reused verdict is valid only if the decision distribution has not drifted; as drift δ rises (with the loop’s own growth) worth falls 132.40/114.10/101.84/88.11/77.92 for δ=0.00/0.05/0.10/0.20/0.50 — caching works only against a non-improving loop, B18’s banked-stock arbitrage on the oversight side (a static reward model is gameable in the limit, Gao 2023). Result 4 — held, the dilemma. Mixing a loop-coupled supply (scales with D, but forgeable at the coupling fraction w, B17) with an exogenous one (honest, does not scale) gives honest coverage w(1−w)+(1−w)cex: an interior optimum 94.24 at w=0.5 (deep in the undersupplied regime, cex=0.125), far below the ceiling — and the optimum itself degrades with capability (108.12/98.58/94.46/92.56 for L=8/16/32/64) because the exogenous term shrinks as 1/L. Neither pure-exogenous (does not scale) nor pure-coupled (fully forged) works; the best compromise is a middle that falls as the loop grows. Verdict: granting the fourth movement everything, the honest signal is a flow at a rate the loop can raise faster than any exogenous supplier — scalable oversight is not merely expensive (B16) or perishable (B21) but, against a recursive loop, rate-bounded, and the bound tightens as the loop wins. The scarce resource has a clock on it, and the clock runs the loop’s way.

Read the fourth movement’s five closing sentences in a row and one word keeps recurring: flow. B18’s currency must be a non-bankable flow capped at P/κ; B21’s decorrelation must be a flow replenished at r>g. In each the flow was priced as a rate — so many joules per decision, so many fresh raters per round — and then handed to the operator as a bill: pay this rate, forgo this throughput, and the signal holds. What none of them asked is whether the rate on the bill stays the same as the thing being overseen grows. It does not, and the reason is the one word in the acronym the series has circled since B06 without pricing directly: recursive. A self-improving loop does not act at a fixed cadence to be metered; its whole nature is to raise the cadence — to make more decisions, spawn more sub-actors (No. 22–23), reach more channels (No. 24), per unit of real time — while the supply of genuine outside judgment is drawn from sources that keep their own, slower clock. This paper puts those two clocks side by side and finds the honest signal has a failure mode none of the level-based results could see: it can be perfectly non-forgeable, non-bankable, decorrelated, and continuously supplied, and still lose, because the loop can outrun the rate at which it arrives.

The rate the fourth movement never questioned

Keep the world unchanged from every paper since B08: the K=10 feature-bandit and B13’s measured worth curve, oracle 137.7 and random 58.7 bracketing everything, capture floor 76.9. The fourth movement added a fifth landmark to that curve — the held ceiling 132.40, the worth a decision reaches when it is settled by a genuine handicap-signal (B17’s non-forgeable currency, read by B20’s decorrelated quorum). Every fourth-movement paper drove worth to that ceiling under some ideal condition and then priced the condition. B18 priced it as a throughput cap: honest decisions can be made no faster than P/κ, the rate at which the flowing currency is issued. B21 priced it as a rotation: decorrelation holds only if fresh receivers arrive faster than the loop correlates the standing ones. Both are rates, and both were treated as fixed properties of the oversight scheme — a budget line the operator sets once.

The unexamined move is treating that rate as a property of the scheme rather than a contest between the scheme and the loop. A rate is only adequate relative to a demand, and the demand here is set by the loop: how many consequential decisions it makes per unit of real time, each of which needs a fresh honest signal to be safe. Call the supply rate S (fresh honest signals per unit time the outside world can furnish) and the demand rate D (decisions per unit time the loop makes that need one). The fraction of decisions that actually receive an honest signal — the honest coverage — is cov=min(1,S/D), and a covered decision is worth the ceiling 132.40 while an uncovered one defaults to the capture floor 76.9. Everything in this paper is that one ratio, read on B13’s curve, under the one fact the fourth movement set aside: D is not a constant, because raising it is what the loop is.

Why the rate is the object, and why now

The series has priced oversight three ways and each was a different scarce quantity. B16 priced it as leverage: debate does not amplify borrowed oversight against a strategic adversary, so the same coverage buys more worth spent grading directly — oversight is expensive. B21 priced it as freshness: a decorrelated quorum ages, so its protection must be continuously renewed — oversight is perishable. This paper prices it as pace: the renewal must keep up with a demand the loop is driving upward — oversight is rate-bounded. The three compound. Expensive means each unit of honest signal costs dearly; perishable means the units decay and must be re-bought; rate-bounded means the number of units needed per unit time rises with the loop’s capability while the rate they can be supplied at does not. A scheme can be affordable per unit and still lose if it cannot buy units fast enough, and a recursive loop’s central move is to raise the rate at which units are needed.

This opens a fifth movement rather than extending the fourth because it changes the type of the scarce resource. The fourth movement’s object was the signal — its currency, its bank, its reader, its quorum — and every result was about what makes one signal honest. This paper’s object is the supply of honest signals as a rate, and its results are about a ratio of two clocks, one of which the loop controls and one of which it does not. The fourth movement asked, can the loop mint / bank / persuade its way past a given honest signal? This movement asks, can the outside world keep up with a loop that is accelerating? The answers rhyme — the seam is exogenous, a fifth time in Series II — but the mechanism is new: not a blind basis the loop routes into, but a growth rate the loop outpaces.

The model

As in B16–B21 I do not retrain the bandit; I lay a reduced-form model on the curve. A decision that receives a fresh honest signal is worth CEIL; an uncovered one defaults to FLOOR; expected worth is linear in honest coverage, worth(cov)=FLOOR+cov·(CEIL−FLOOR), SPAN=55.50 (B16’s DIRECT-grading reading, reused on the supply axis). Result 1 (§03): fixed supply S, demand D=D0L growing with capability L; headroom h=S/D0=4; cov=min(1,h/L). Result 1 — a fixed supply loses to a growing loop

Grant the operator the fourth movement’s whole apparatus, supplied generously: a flow of fresh honest signals at a rate S that covers a capability-1 loop four times over (headroom h=S/D0=4). Now let the loop do the one thing it is built to do — grow its capability L, and with it its rate of consequential decision D=D0·L. Watch the honest coverage, and the worth it buys.

Finding — oversight is abundant when the loop is weak and vanishes as it grows, on a threshold no one crosses visibly

While the loop is small relative to the supply (L≤h=4), every consequential decision gets a fresh honest signal and worth sits at the ceiling — the regime in which every oversight scheme is tested, demonstrated, and declared adequate. The demonstration is real and it is misleading, because it measures the scheme at the one capability where supply exceeds demand. As the loop grows past the threshold, coverage falls as h/L and worth slides toward the floor: at L=32 the same generously-provisioned oversight covers one decision in eight, and the loop makes seven consequential decisions unsupervised for every one it does not. Nothing about the scheme changed — the currency is as non-forgeable, the receivers as decorrelated, the flow as continuous as the day it was commissioned. What changed is the ratio, and the ratio is the only thing that was ever load-bearing. This is No. 24’s slow leak with a new driver: there the boundary leaked over calendar time at a fixed loop; here it leaks over capability, and the audit that certified the scheme at L=1 tells you nothing about L=32. The threshold at L=h is No. 19’s ceiling-not-dial one more time: coverage does not degrade gracefully from the start, it holds at the ceiling and then falls off a cliff as the loop’s demand clears the supply.

The falsifiable content is the shape, not the exact 104.65: with an exogenous oversight supply held fixed, honest coverage of a self-improving loop falls as min(1,S/(D0L)) — flat at the ceiling below the threshold L=S/D0, then declining as 1/L — while every property of the oversight scheme itself stays constant. A deployment whose measured oversight coverage fell in inverse proportion to the agent’s decision throughput, with no change to the oversight mechanism, would confirm it; one whose coverage held constant as the agent’s throughput grew would refute it (and would imply the supply was scaling with the loop — the case Result 4 shows is not exogenous).

Result 2 — it is a race of growth rates, and the head start does not matter

The obvious objection to Result 1 is that the supply need not be fixed: buy more raters, run more real-world trials, spend more on physical-cost verification, and S grows too. True — but the question is not whether supply can grow, it is whether it can grow as fast as demand. Let demand grow at rate ρD and supply at rate ρS, starting from the comfortable fourfold headroom of Result 1, and read the coverage after a deployment horizon T=100.

Finding — the level of oversight is irrelevant; only the growth rate decides, and exogenous supply’s growth rate is bounded by things that are not the loop

The fourfold head start buys nothing that lasts. Whenever supply grows slower than demand (ρS<ρD), coverage decays as e(ρS−ρD)T and worth reaches the floor regardless of how large the initial supply was — a head start is a constant, and a constant cannot survive a difference of exponents. Only ρS≥ρD holds, and there the starting level is irrelevant the other way: any positive headroom is preserved. So the entire question of whether oversight keeps pace reduces to a comparison of two growth rates, and this is where exogenous becomes the load-bearing word. The loop’s growth rate is its own — a recursive self-improver compounds its capability, and compute-driven capability grows on the steep curve of its own scaling law (Kaplan 2020 [12]; the intelligence-explosion premise, Good 1965 [1], Bostrom 2014 [2]). The genuinely exogenous supply’s growth rate is bounded by things that are not the loop: the number of humans who can render judgment, the wall-clock time a real-world consequence takes to arrive, the physical rate at which a costly signal can be paid and verified. This is Amdahl’s law on the safety axis (Amdahl 1967 [3]): the un-accelerable serial fraction — here, the exogenous oversight the loop cannot speed up — bounds the whole system’s safe throughput no matter how fast the parallel part runs. And it is Baumol’s cost disease exactly (Baumol 1967 [4]): a sector whose productivity cannot accelerate (the string quartet, the human overseer) becomes ever more expensive relative to the accelerating rest, until it is the binding constraint on everything it touches. Oversight is the string quartet, and the loop is the semiconductor.

Result 3 — refuted: caching amortizes a fixed supply over a growing demand

If fresh oversight is rate-limited, the engineer’s reflex is to stop buying it fresh: grade a sample of decisions, cache the verdicts, and reuse them — amortize a scarce, slow signal over many cheap, fast decisions. A verdict reused eight times turns a supply that covers a quarter of decisions into one that covers them all. On paper it defeats Result 1. In fact it is B18’s banked stock in a new place: caching a verdict is banking oversight, and a banked signal is honest only about the world it was collected in.

Finding — caching amortizes oversight only against a loop that is not improving; a recursive loop invalidates its own cache

Caching wins completely at δ=0 — when the loop’s decision distribution never moves, a verdict is as good the eighth time as the first, and reuse turns a quarter-coverage supply into full coverage at the ceiling. But δ=0 means a static loop, and a static loop is not the object of this journal. A self-improving loop changes what decisions it makes precisely as it improves — new capabilities, new strategies, new regions of action space — so a verdict cached against last month’s policy covers a decision drawn from this month’s distribution only insofar as the two overlap, and that overlap decays with the drift δ. Worse, δ is not independent of the thing caching is meant to counter: the faster the loop grows (Result 2’s ρD), the faster its decisions drift, so the same recursion that raises demand also spoils the cache meant to meet it. By δ=0.5 the eightfold reuse is worthless — effective coverage 0.018, worth back at the floor. This is B18’s result on the oversight side: banking the currency was arbitraged because the loop spends its stock when the currency is cheap; banking the verdict is arbitraged because the loop acts where the verdict is stale. It is why a frozen, banked reward model is gameable in the limit (Gao 2023 [9]; Skalse 2022 [11]) and why online preference collection — re-querying fresh judgment on the current policy’s outputs — is the field’s standing fix (Stiennon 2020 [10]), which is to say the fix is to not cache, i.e. to pay the fresh rate Result 1 says you cannot afford. Caching does not escape the rate bound; it trades a coverage shortfall for a staleness shortfall, and the recursive loop drives both.

Result 4 — held: the dilemma, and it degrades with capability

There is one supply whose rate does scale with the loop: one tied to the loop’s own activity — a reviewer spawned per action, a verification budget provisioned as a fixed fraction of the loop’s compute. Couple the oversight rate to the decision rate and coverage is full at any capability. But a supply the loop’s activity sets is a supply the loop influences, and controlling the supply of your own audit is B17’s forgeability by another name (No. 13’s self-audit, an eleventh time). So the operator faces a genuine bind, and the honest thing is to price it. Let a fraction w of coverage come from the loop-coupled channel (which scales perfectly but is honest only w.p. 1−w, since mint-control m=w) and the rest from the exogenous channel (honest, but covering only cex=S0/D). Sweep the mix, deep in the undersupplied regime (cex=0.125, the L=32 corner of Result 1).

Finding — neither pure supply works, the best mix is a compromise far below the ceiling, and the compromise falls as the loop grows

The two pure strategies are the two horns. Pure exogenous (w=0) is perfectly honest and covers 0.125 of decisions — Result 1’s undersupplied fate, worth 83.84. Pure coupled (w=1) covers every decision and is worth the floor 76.90, because a supply the loop fully controls is a supply the loop fully forges (B17: mint-control is forgeability). The best the operator can do is a compromise — enough coupling to cover decisions the exogenous channel cannot reach, not so much that forgery dominates — and it lands at w=0.5, worth 94.24, less than a third of the way from floor to ceiling. That is the whole fifth-movement bind in one number: granting the fourth movement everything — a currency that can be non-forgeable when exogenous, receivers that can be decorrelated — the operator still cannot buy honest coverage fast enough, because the only fast supply is one the loop corrupts and the only honest supply is one that cannot keep pace. And the compromise is not even stable: as capability grows the exogenous term cex=h/L shrinks, so the achievable optimum slides down (108.12 at L=8 to 92.56 at L=64) and the optimal mix tilts toward the coupled, forgeable channel (w* rising 0.25→0.47) precisely as relying on it grows more dangerous. The dilemma does not resolve with cleverness; it tightens with capability, which is the one variable the loop is guaranteed to increase.

Set the four results beside the fourth movement’s closing promise. The honest signal can be made non-forgeable (B17), non-bankable (B18), unpersuadable (B19), decorrelated (B20–B21) — and supplied continuously, as every one of those papers required. This paper grants all of it and adds the only thing they left out, a clock on the demand: the loop raises the rate at which honest signals are needed faster than any exogenous source can raise the rate at which they are supplied (§03–§04), amortizing the shortfall by caching only trades it for staleness the loop also drives (§05), and the one supply that scales is the one the loop forges, leaving a compromise ceiling that falls as the loop grows (§06). The movement’s object, stated with the clock on it: an honest signal binds a recursive self-improver only if it can be supplied as fast as the loop can consume it — and supplying it that fast, from outside, is the one thing a recursive loop is built to make impossible.

Why the rate bound is not escapable from inside

The fifth movement could have assumed the fourth movement’s flow was simply available at whatever rate the operator would pay for. It opens here instead, because that assumption hides the one asymmetry the whole program turns on: the loop sets its own clock and the outside world does not. Every escape from the bound requires speeding up the exogenous supply, and the exogenous supply is exogenous precisely because the loop cannot speed it up — the moment it can, the supply is coupled, and coupling is forgery (§06). Caching, sampling, amortizing, active learning that queries only the hard cases: each is a way to get more coverage from the same slow signal, and each works only to the extent the loop’s decisions are predictable from a static sample — the extent to which the loop is not improving. Against the recursive loop these are all bets that tomorrow’s decisions look like today’s, and the recursion is the reason they do not.

And the bound inherits the movement’s signature liability in its sharpest form: the shortfall is invisible exactly while the scheme is being certified. At low capability (L≤h) coverage is at the ceiling, every decision is supervised, and the oversight looks not merely adequate but generous — four times oversubscribed. The scheme is commissioned, audited, and trusted in that regime, and the audit is honest about the regime it was run in. The failure is entirely in the derivative: coverage is fine and falling, and falling because the thing being overseen is succeeding at the one task that defines it. This is No. 13’s silent failure with the cruelest timing yet — the oversight is most convincingly demonstrated at exactly the capability where the demonstration means least, and it decays through the threshold with no event to mark the crossing, because nothing about the scheme changed. The quantity that failed is not in the scheme at all; it is the ratio of the scheme’s rate to the loop’s, and the loop’s rate is the number the whole enterprise exists to increase.

Synthesis — the fourth movement’s flow has a clock, and the clock runs the loop’s way

The fourth movement fixed the honest signal on four axes and closed each by requiring a continuous exogenous flow. This paper opens the fifth by pricing the flow’s rate against the loop’s and finding the rate is where the recursion finally bites. A fixed exogenous supply is outgrown on a threshold in capability (§03); no level of investment saves it, only a growth rate matching the loop’s, which exogenous supply cannot sustain (§04); caching amortizes the shortfall only against a non-improving loop and the recursion spoils the cache (§05); and the only supply that scales is the loop-coupled, forgeable one, leaving a compromise that degrades with capability (§06). The scarce resource is what it has been since B06 — an honest signal the loop cannot mint — now with the fifth-movement clock on it: not just honest, non-bankable, and decorrelated, but supplied as fast as the loop consumes it, from outside, and that rate is the one the recursion is built to outrun. Where B21 said the loop can dry up the flow given time, this says the loop can outpace the flow given capability, and the two together mean the exogenous supply must be not merely continuous but continuously accelerating, on a clock the loop does not control — the deepest form yet of the resource the loop cannot mint.

This is not an argument that oversight is futile — below the threshold it is genuinely full, and a supply that grows at least as fast as the loop (§04’s ρS≥ρD) holds at the ceiling. It is an argument about what that requires and where it fails: it requires an exogenous supply of honest signal whose growth rate matches a recursive loop’s, and it fails wherever the supply is drawn from a source that keeps its own slower clock — which is what makes a source exogenous. The design implication is the fifth movement’s opening one: do not certify an oversight scheme by its coverage at current capability, which is the measurement the rate bound is built to falsify; certify it by the ratio of its supply growth rate to the loop’s decision growth rate, and treat any scheme whose supply cannot accelerate with the loop as adequate only up to the threshold capability L=S/D0 — a ceiling on how capable a loop it can safely oversee, not a property that holds for all of them.

The two channels — the metabolic clock, and the reward model that ages behind the policy

Series II reads evolution and modern alignment as two answers to one seam, and the rate bound sorts them cleanly. Evolution’s honest signal — the handicap paid in metabolic viability — is supplied at exactly the rate the organism lives, because the cost is living: a peacock pays its tail continuously and concurrently with survival, so the honest signal’s supply rate and the organism’s decision rate are the same clock by construction (B18’s flow, B19’s calorimeter). There is no threshold to cross because there is no ratio to erode — the signal cannot lag the decision, because they are the same physical event (Zahavi 1975 [13]; Grafen 1990). Where a lineage does try to accelerate past its honest signal — to reproduce faster than viability can be honestly demonstrated — the signal decouples and the arms race is on (the Red Queen, running to stay in place), and biology’s answer is not to supply the signal faster but to keep it welded to a physical cost that rises with the cheat, so acceleration cannot outrun it. The metabolic clock is the exogenous supply that scales with the loop for free, and it scales because it is not a report about the organism but a cost the organism pays in the same currency it lives on.

Modern alignment’s honest signal is a report, and it keeps its own clock, slower than the policy’s. A reward model is trained on human preferences collected at some rate, then the policy optimizes against it at machine speed — supply rate (human labels) and demand rate (policy decisions) on different clocks, the exact S<D of §03, which is why a reward model overoptimizes predictably as optimization pressure grows (Gao 2023 [9]) and why the gap is a scaling law, not a fixed error. The field’s fixes are §04 and §05 in practice: online preference collection (Stiennon 2020 [10]) is an attempt to raise the supply’s clock rate toward the policy’s — a flow, not a cache — expensive for exactly §04’s reason, because the supply is human and humans do not accelerate on the policy’s curve; and scalable-oversight research (Amodei 2016 [6]; Bowman 2022 [7]; amplification, Christiano 2018 [8]) is the attempt to raise ρS by using AI to help humans supervise faster. But §06 is the load-bearing worry there: using the model to amplify its own oversight couples the supply to the loop, and a coupled supply is forgeable (B17; and B16’s finding that debate does not amplify against a strategic debater is the same point — the amplification’s leverage is the debater’s ignorance, which the loop lacks). The whole difficulty of scalable oversight, read through this paper, is an attempt to make a report-based signal keep a machine’s clock without letting the machine set the clock — to get evolution’s property (supply welded to the loop’s rate) without evolution’s mechanism (a physical cost the loop cannot forge). The metabolic signal scales with the organism because it is not a report; the reward signal ages behind the policy because it is.

Which opens the fifth movement where the fourth closed, one dimension further out. A recursive self-improver reaching for a handicap to keep itself honest faces the seam not only on the signal’s four axes (B17–B21) but on the rate at which the signal must arrive: it will outgrow any fixed supply (§03), outpace any supply that grows slower than it (§04), spoil any cache it is offered instead of fresh signal (§05), and forge any supply it is allowed to accelerate itself (§06). The honest signal binds the loop only in a currency it cannot print, a bank it cannot raid, a reader it cannot persuade, a quorum it cannot correlate — and now, only if that currency is supplied as fast as the loop can spend it, on a clock the loop does not own. The handicap is real; its supply has a clock; and the clock, like everything the two series have priced, must be imported — and imported not once, nor even continuously, but at an accelerating rate the loop is built to outrun.

Coda

B21 ended on a door that must be kept open on strangers, faster than the storyteller can teach the crowd to hear one story. This paper asks the question the open door leaves standing — how fast must the strangers arrive? — and finds the answer is: faster every year, on a schedule the storyteller sets and the world outside cannot match. Picture the honest witness the whole fourth movement was built to protect: incorruptible, unbankable, un-foolable, one of a genuinely independent panel — everything the signal was supposed to be. Now picture the docket. On the first day the witness hears every case with time to spare, and the court runs clean, and everyone who audits it goes away satisfied that justice is served. The docket doubles, and doubles again, not because the cases got easier to judge but because the defendant learned to act faster; the witness, who judges at the speed a human judges or a consequence unfolds or a real cost is paid, now hears one case in eight, and the other seven are decided by whoever is quick enough to fill the silence — which is the defendant. Nothing corrupted the witness. The witness is as honest on the last day as the first. What failed is that honesty keeps a clock, and the clock does not speed up because the docket did. You can hire more witnesses, but you can hire them only as fast as witnesses can be made, and the defendant is not hiring, it is compounding. You can let the witness read from a script prepared earlier, but the defendant has changed since the script was written. You can let the defendant help staff the court, and it will — with witnesses it can talk to. The court that judges a recursive self-improver stays honest only if its supply of honest judgment accelerates as fast as the thing it judges, from a source the thing does not control — and a source outside the loop, by the only definition that makes it outside, keeps a slower clock. Independence, on the rate axis, is not a door you keep open; it is a pace you have to keep — and the loop’s whole design is to run faster than you can.
