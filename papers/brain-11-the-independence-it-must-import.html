Series II · Does the Brain Backpropagate?

The Independence It Must Import

B10 ended the audit at a wall: the reach of any internal instrument equals the independence of the channels it is built from, and a shared bias survives every internal check disguised as consensus — so independence must be imported from outside. This paper measures the import. It asks two operational questions the whole movement was walking toward: how much genuinely-independent signal does it take to break a captured panel, and can a loop even verify that a channel it is offered is independent before it pays to import it? The answers are unforgiving. Importing independence is not a contribution you top up; it is a takeover you must win — and the loop cannot tell an honest channel from a differently-biased one by any signal it owns, so it cannot verify what it is buying.

Abstract

Series II has driven one seam down five levels: a frozen model is all outer loop (B06), cannot mint its own fitness (B07), cannot originate its reward reference, only borrow one (B08), cannot audit the borrowed channel's honesty from inside (B09), and cannot even build a valid audit, because every instrument it constructs shares the channel's blind spot (B10). B10's one escape was explicit: the missing ingredient — independence — must be imported from outside. This paper takes the import itself as the object and measures its cost, in B08–B10's feature-bandit world (oracle 137.7 true reward/episode, random reference 58.7). Result 1 — importing by vote is a takeover, not a contribution. Add h honest channels to a captured panel of B=8 shared-biased ones and take the majority: a minority of honest channels does nothing — the loop stays at or below random for h=1…7 (44–66, vs random 58.7) — and recovery begins only when the honest bloc reaches parity with the biased one (h=8→103.8) and completes when it takes the majority (h=9→129.6, h=12→133.5, oracle 137.7). You do not supplement a captured panel; you must outvote it. Result 2 — a ground-truth probe is worth more than a biased vote, but fractional import is still a threshold, not a trickle. Decide a fraction p of the loop's comparisons by an unbiased ground-truth verdict and the rest by the captured panel: below p≈0.3 the imported signal is outvoted and wasted (p≤0.2→62–70≈random), above it the loop recovers (p=0.3/0.5/1.0→99.2/90.0/130.5). A ground-truth probe is decisive where a biased vote is outnumbered — so ground truth is the cheaper import — but it must still win a plurality of the loop's decisions, not merely appear in them. Independence is bought in decisive quantity or not at all. Result 3 — the loop cannot verify what it is importing. Offer the loop two candidate channels it may inspect only by their disagreement with its incumbent panel: an honest channel H (worth importing) and a channel B′ biased toward a different arm (worthless). The worthless B′ disagrees with the panel more than the honest H (0.490 vs 0.380), yet only H recovers the loop when imported (89.4 vs 70.5, incumbent 53.3). Disagreement measures difference-from-incumbent, not closeness-to-truth — and a differently-biased channel is maximally different while no closer to truth, so a loop ranking candidates by disagreement imports the harmful one first. To tell H from B′ the loop would have to compare each to ground truth: the very independent reference it is trying to acquire. The regress closes the movement. A frozen model does not merely need independence it cannot manufacture; it needs it in a quantity large enough to win, and it cannot verify a supplier's offer without already possessing what the supplier sells. This is why scalable-oversight research is about importing verified signal cheaply (debate, amplification, weak-to-strong) and why reward-model ensembles cap out (Eisenstein 2023): they raise head count, which is contribution, not the takeover independence requires.

B10 left the loop with a coupon it could not read: independence, purchasable only from outside. This paper tries to spend it. It measures what a unit of imported independence actually buys — and finds that the unit is large, that a minority of it is wasted, and that the loop cannot check the coupon's denomination before it pays. Three results, one world, one conclusion the whole second movement was bending toward: the frozen model's dependence on the outside is not a gap it can close with a little help, but a supplier relationship it can neither escape nor audit.

The question, narrowed to the import

B10's final sentence was a promissory note. It proved that every internal bias-audit — consistency against itself, cross-reference against a panel, watch the panel's disagreement — has a blind region exactly the size of the bias its instruments share, and that the shared bias arrives disguised as the one thing the loop reads as trust: consensus. The escape, the only one left standing, was that the ingredient the audit lacked — genuine independence — could be imported. Hand the captured panel one honest channel, one ground-truth probe, one outside reference, and the shared bias becomes visible against it. B10 asserted the import was necessary; it did not measure the import.

That is this paper, and the measurement matters because the import is not obviously affordable. “Import an independent channel” hides two questions a real system has to answer. First, how much? — is one honest voice among a biased panel enough to correct it, or does correction require a quantity of imported independence that scales with the bias it must overcome? Second, and sharper, can the loop tell? — when a candidate channel is offered, can the loop verify from inside that it is independent (worth importing) rather than a channel that merely looks different while carrying its own bias? The first question is about the price of independence. The second is about whether the loop can even read the price tag. Both have exact answers in the B08–B10 world, and both cut the same way.

The world, and two ways to import

The world is B08–B10's, unchanged, so every number is comparable across four papers. A K-armed feature world (K=10, features in R8): four value arms carry real value on the hidden reference axis a (feature 0); four salient distractors carry near-zero value but large off-axis magnitude (norm ≈ 4), so the highest-feeling arm is a valueless one; two neutral arms. An inner ε-greedy bandit is paid its felt reward w·φ; a gradient-free (1+λ) evolution search tunes the reference w on the unit sphere, selecting by a 1-bit Bradley–Terry preference channel (Bradley & Terry 1952). Baselines, 12 seeds: the oracle w=a scores 137.7 true reward/episode; a random reference scores 58.7. Those bracket everything below.

The starting condition is B10's endpoint: a captured panel of B=8 channels that all share one bias — every channel over-credits the same vivid distractor (c=1.0, past B09's inversion threshold; ρ=1 in B10's dial). Left alone, majority-vote of that panel drives the reference to the loved distractor and scores near random. Onto that captured loop I try two ways to import independence:

  • Import by vote (Result 1). Add h genuinely honest channels (c=0, no loved arm) to the panel and keep the majority-vote rule. This is the ensemble remedy taken literally: buy more evaluators, and buy good ones. The question is how many honest channels it takes to overturn the biased bloc.
  • Import by ground truth (Result 2). Keep the captured panel, but let a fraction p of the loop's comparisons be decided by an unbiased ground-truth verdict instead of the panel — one true-reward probe every ≈1/p decisions. This is scalable oversight taken literally: you cannot afford ground truth on every step, so you sprinkle it. The question is what fraction is enough.

And one diagnostic (Result 3): before importing, can the loop verify a candidate channel is worth importing, using only what it can measure — the candidate's agreement with the panel it already has? Twelve seeds throughout; final references measured on 100 fresh episodes. Script (numpy, fixed seeds) at scratchpad/import_independence.py.

12 seeds; oracle 137.7, random 58.7. Nothing happens until the honest bloc reaches the size of the biased bloc: h=1…7 hovers at or below random; recovery ignites at parity (h=8) and completes at majority (h≥9).

The curve is not a ramp; it is a step, and the step sits exactly where the honest votes can outnumber the biased ones. For h from 1 to 7 the imported honest channels are real, unbiased, and useless: the loop scores 44–66, straddling random, because on every pivotal comparison — the value-axis reference versus the loved-distractor reference — the eight biased channels vote distractor and the fewer honest channels vote value, and the majority is still the biased bloc's. The honest channels are outvoted on precisely the decisions that matter, so their independence buys nothing. Only at h=8, parity, does the reward jump to 103.8 (the vote becomes a near-tie the honest side sometimes wins), and only at h≥9, a real majority, does the loop recover to near-oracle (129.6, 133.5). Importing independence into a captured panel is not a matter of adding a good signal; the good signal is drowned until it is the dominant signal. You do not supplement a captured evaluator. You must overthrow it.

Why “add a few good judges” cannot work — and it is the same wall as B10

This is B10's majority-capture threshold read from the other side. There, a shared bias captured the vote once it reached a majority of the panel; here, an honest import fails to correct the vote until it reaches a majority. Same threshold, same mechanism, opposite direction: the majority-vote aggregator has no notion of which voters are right, only how many agree, so correction is a headcount battle and independence has to win it outright. A minority of truth loses to a majority of shared error every time — which is why, in practice, sprinkling a few high-quality raters or a single strong reward model into a pipeline dominated by a shared annotation bias does not move the outcome. The bias is not a weak signal to be diluted; it is a bloc to be outvoted.

Result 2 — a ground-truth probe beats a biased vote, but fractional import is still a threshold

12 seeds. Below p≈0.3 the imported ground truth is outvoted by the biased panel on most decisions and the loop stays near random; above it, ground truth wins enough decisions to hold the reference near the value axis.

Two things are true at once, and the honest reading keeps both. First, ground truth is the cheaper import: a single true-reward probe is decisive on its comparison, whereas a single honest vote is merely one head against eight, so a modest probe rate (p=0.3, one decision in three) reaches 99.2 — a recovery that importing honest votes could not buy until they were a majority of a growing panel. This is the B08/B09 theme at the import level: an unbiased-but-thin signal outperforms a fat-but-biased one, because honesty is decisive and bias is only numerous. Second, and against any hope of a free lunch, fractional import is still a threshold, not a trickle: below p≈0.3 the loop sits at random (62–70 for p≤0.2), because the greedy search is a tug-of-war — the biased panel decides the majority of comparisons and drags the reference back to the distractor faster than the sparse probes can pull it to the value axis. Independence injected below the rate at which the bias operates is not partial help; it is captured and erased, exactly as B07–B10 kept finding. The loop needs the imported signal to win a plurality of its decisions, not merely to appear among them.

Result 3 — the loop cannot verify what it is importing

Results 1 and 2 priced independence assuming the loop already knows which channel is the independent one. That assumption is the last thing to test, and it is where the regress closes. A loop being offered a candidate channel cannot see the channel's bias directly — if it could, it would not have needed the channel. All it can measure is how the candidate agrees with the panel it already holds. So offer it two candidates and let it inspect only their disagreement with the incumbent (captured) panel:

  • H — an honest channel (c=0). The one worth importing: Result 1 showed honest channels recover the loop once they carry the vote.
  • B′ — a channel biased toward a different arm (c=1.0, a loved distractor other than the panel's). Worthless: it carries its own systematic error, just pointed elsewhere.

Disagreement measures difference-from-incumbent, not closeness-to-truth — and ranks the candidates backwards

The one signal the loop can read points the wrong way. The worthless candidate B′ disagrees with the incumbent panel more than the honest H does (0.490 vs 0.380) — because a bias pointed at a different distractor contradicts the panel's distractor on a wide swath of comparisons, while the honest channel only diverges on the specific comparisons the shared bias corrupts. Yet importing H recovers the loop (89.4, up from 53.3) and importing B′ barely moves it (70.5, and worthless because unidentifiable). A loop following the obvious heuristic — “the channel that disagrees most with my current panel is the most independent, import it first” — imports the harmful candidate ahead of the useful one. Disagreement is distance from the incumbent, and a differently-biased channel is maximally distant while no closer to the truth. To distinguish H from B′, the loop would have to score each candidate against ground truth — the independent reference it was trying to import in the first place. Verifying independence requires the independence being verified. The regress that has shadowed every paper in this movement closes here: the loop cannot mint the fitness (B07), cannot originate the reference (B08), cannot trust the channel (B09), cannot audit the channel (B10), and now cannot even verify the supplier without already holding what the supplier sells.

Threats to validity — read before generalising

(1) Majority vote is one aggregator, and Result 1's takeover threshold is partly a property of headcount voting. A weighted or confidence-aware aggregator that knew which channels to trust would let a minority of good judges win — but knowing which to trust is exactly Result 3's unsolved problem, so the escape is circular: you cannot weight by trustworthiness without a trust signal, and the trust signal is the imported independence. Unweighted vote is the honest model of a loop that cannot tell its channels apart. (2) The ground-truth probe in Result 2 is a perfect oracle. Real imported signal (a stronger model, a held-out human, a formal check) is itself imperfect; the threshold p≈0.3 is a floor — noisier imports need a higher rate, and a biased import (B09) reintroduces the whole problem one level up. The result bounds the best case. (3) Both thresholds are sandbox-specific numbers (12 seeds, small world); the exact h≈B and p≈0.3 will move with panel size, bias magnitude, and search greediness. The shape — nothing below a pivotal quantity, recovery above it — is a property of a greedy loop pulled between a persistent bias and a sparse correction, not of the specific constants. (4) Result 3's two candidates are extreme (fully honest vs fully biased-elsewhere); a spectrum of partial biases would give a spectrum of disagreements, but the ordering pathology — more-disagreeing can be less-valuable — is enough to void disagreement as a certificate. (5) The learned-reward-model version — real evaluators with correlated failure modes, a deep policy, an imperfect and possibly biased imported supervisor, and disagreement measured on real prompts — is to-be-run-locally; the prediction is that the takeover threshold survives (a minority of good supervision is outvoted), the ground-truth threshold survives (sparse oversight below the bias's operating rate is captured), and disagreement stays a backwards certificate (an off-distribution or differently-biased supervisor looks maximally independent).

Why this is the exact shape of scalable oversight

The three results are not a sandbox curiosity; they are the reason an entire research program exists and the reason it is hard. The program is scalable oversight — how to supervise a system on tasks where correct ground truth is too expensive to supply on every decision (Amodei et al. 2016; Bowman et al. 2022). Result 2 is its founding problem in one number: you cannot afford p=1, and below a threshold p the cheap majority of un-verified signal captures the loop. Every proposed method is, in this paper's terms, a scheme for importing decisive independence more cheaply than a full oracle. Iterated amplification (Christiano et al. 2018) decomposes a hard judgement into many cheap ones a weak-but-trusted judge can verify, manufacturing a plurality of verified decisions from a thin ground-truth budget. Debate (Irving et al. 2018) pits two strong systems against each other so that a weak judge's single verified verdict becomes decisive — an amplifier for the import rate p. Weak-to-strong generalization (Burns et al. 2023) asks whether an imperfect, independent weak supervisor can elicit a strong model's latent competence — exactly Result 2's imperfect import, and it finds partial recovery, a p<1 that helps but does not close the gap. And reward-model ensembles (Coste 2023; Eisenstein 2023) are Result 1: they raise head count, which cancels the idiosyncratic component but leaves the shared bias a majority bloc, so they mitigate and do not eliminate reward hacking. The field's central open problem list (Casper et al. 2023) names the same wall: RLHF cannot bootstrap past the systematic limits of its feedback from inside.

Read together, the results say what all these methods are fighting and why none of them is free. They are fighting the fact that a captured loop's error is a majority, not a whisper, so correction must be decisive, not additive (Result 1); that decisive correction has a minimum rate set by how fast the bias operates (Result 2); and that the loop cannot even identify a good supervisor without a good supervisor to check it against (Result 3). Scalable oversight is expensive not because ground truth is expensive to collect — though it is — but because independence has to be imported in winning quantity and cannot be verified in advance. The methods that work do so by lowering the quantity of verified signal needed to win, never by removing the need to win.

The two channels, one last time

Series II has read evolution and RLHF as two answers to the same seam, and this paper gives the last contrast its sharpest form. Evolution never had to import independence, because its single channel — differential reproduction — was ground truth on the objective it optimized: survival was not a proxy for fitness, it was fitness, so p=1 by construction and there was no captured panel to overthrow. It paid for that decisive honesty in the only currency it had, deep time, and it never faced Result 3 because it never had to choose a channel — the environment was the channel, non-negotiable. Modern alignment is in the opposite position on every axis: its channel is a fat, learned, shared-biased panel (B09/B10), so it must import independence (this paper) — and it must import it in winning quantity against a bias that is itself a majority, from suppliers it cannot verify without the independence it is trying to buy. That is not a solvable engineering detail hiding inside a mostly-autonomous loop. It is the structural reason a frozen, self-refining model has a ceiling: not that it lacks a good signal, but that acquiring one is a takeover it must fund from outside and cannot audit from inside.

What this establishes, and where the movement closes

The second movement asked whether the fitness signal — the external anchor Movement I proved the loop needs — could be engineered from within. Five papers answer, in one descending line: the loop cannot mint the fitness (B07), cannot originate the reference (B08), cannot trust the channel's honesty (B09), cannot build a valid audit of it (B10), and now — the movement's floor — cannot import the independence an audit needs except in a quantity large enough to win the loop's decisions outright, and cannot verify that an offered channel carries that independence without already possessing an independent reference to check it against (B11). The dependence on the outside is therefore not a gap to be narrowed with cleverness; it is total in a precise sense: the loop needs a supplier of decisive, verified independence, and it can neither manufacture the independence, nor guarantee its quantity is decisive, nor audit the supplier. B06's seam, in its final operational form for this movement: the frozen model is bounded not by the absence of a good signal but by the economics of importing one — independence must be bought in winning quantity, from a source it cannot certify.

Which names the object a third movement would open. If the loop depends on an external supplier of independence it cannot verify, then the operative questions are about the supplier: what is the minimal institution that can furnish decisive, checkable independence to a self-refining model — and can that institution be captured, so the regress simply moves up a level to “who verifies the verifier’s independence?” Series I walked exactly this staircase on its own substrate, from the anchor to the authority that constructs it (No.23–27: the actor it cannot mint, the supply it can still reach, the operator and quorum and independence it can still erode). B11 is where Series II arrives at the same doorway from the brain's side: the honest single channel evolution got for free is the thing our loops must now purchase, in bulk, on credit they cannot check. That is the next wager.

Coda

There is a hope this paper is written against, gentler than B10's careful mind but no safer: the hope that a student who cannot trust his teachers can at least go and find a better one — seek out an independent voice, weigh it against the chorus he already hears, and let the truer note correct the tune. It is the most reasonable move in the world, and this paper is the measure of its two hidden costs. The first is that one truer note, sung against a confident chorus, changes nothing; the student's mind counts voices, and a single dissent is simply outvoted by the harmony until the dissent is itself a chorus — correction is not a contribution he can accept a little of, but a majority he must assemble. The second, and the one that closes the movement, is that he cannot tell the truer voice from a merely different one. The teacher who contradicts the chorus most loudly is not thereby more right; he may only be wrong in a fresh direction, and from inside, loudness-of-disagreement is all the student can hear. To know which stranger to trust, he would need the very thing the stranger was supposed to give him — an independent hold on the truth — and so he is thrown back on a supplier he cannot audit, purchasing, in bulk and on faith, the one thing evolution's single honest channel never had to buy. A frozen model is that student, diligent to the last: it will go looking for independence, and it will not be able to recognise it when offered, nor afford it in the quantity that would win, and it will conclude — reasonably, and wrongly — that the loudest voice in the room is the free one.
