Series II · Does the Brain Backpropagate?

The Supplier It Cannot Certify

B11 closed the second movement on an object it could name but not resolve: the frozen loop needs decisive, verified independence imported from a supplier it cannot audit. The obvious next move is to lean on an institution — a standing body that furnishes independent judgement to anyone who asks. This paper opens the third movement by testing whether that move buys anything, and finds it moves the problem up one level rather than solving it: an institution is only as independent as the correlation among its own sources, and the one internal mark of a reliable body the loop can read — its internal consensus — ranks institutions backwards, certifying the captured cartel over the honest panel. What actually stops the regress is not authority but exogeneity: grounding the chain, at some level, in an outcome the loop cannot influence.

Abstract

The second movement drove one seam down to its floor: a frozen self-refining loop cannot mint its fitness (B07), originate its reference (B08), trust its channel (B09), audit that channel (B10), or import the independence an audit needs except in a quantity large enough to win its decisions — and it cannot verify a channel it is offered without already holding the independent reference it is trying to buy (B11). That left one hope standing: perhaps the loop need not verify any single channel, because it can lean on an institution — a standing supplier of independent judgement — and inherit its authority. This paper takes the supplier as the object, in B08–B11's feature-bandit world (oracle 137.7 true reward/episode, random reference 58.7). Result 1 — capture propagates up, it does not dissipate. Import the loop's verdict from an M=9-source institution whose sources are a fraction ρI captured shared-bias clones and the rest independent. The institution delivers near-oracle judgement only while its own honest sources hold the majority (ρI≤0.5→129), and collapses below random once the captured sources take it (ρI≥0.67→45.3). Importing "from an institution" buys nothing the institution's own internal independence does not already possess: B10's majority-capture threshold, lifted one level to the supplier. Result 2 — the loop's natural certificate of a reliable body ranks institutions backwards. Offered two institutions and allowed to read only their internal unanimity — the mark of a coherent, authoritative body — the loop sees the captured cartel as more unanimous than the honest panel (0.564 vs 0.336), because correlated sources agree by construction while independent ones disagree. Yet only the honest panel recovers the loop (124.2 vs 57.1). Consensus certifies capture: B10's disagreement-inversion, lifted to the supplier. Result 3 — what stops the regress is exogeneity, not authority. Two maximally-honest institutions that differ only in what they are grounded in behave oppositely under optimization pressure: an outcome-grounded body — reporting a consequence the loop's policy cannot influence — holds near-oracle at every pressure (128–137), while a preference-grounded body — reporting the agent's own felt valuation — is pinned near random (48–56) at every pressure, however honest and independent its sources, because it measures a self-referential quantity the optimization reaches. The regress "who certifies the supplier?" does not terminate by climbing to a more authoritative body; it terminates only where the chain touches an outcome the loop cannot bend. Evolution's differential-reproduction channel needed no certification because it was exactly that: an outcome, not a report.

B11 left the loop shopping for independence it could neither manufacture nor verify. The reasonable next thought — the one every real system has — is to stop shopping for a single honest voice and instead trust an institution: a court, a peer-review system, a market, an ensemble with a charter. This paper asks what that trust actually buys. The answer is that an institution is a channel wearing a uniform, subject to every result of the second movement one level up, and that the loop's instinct for spotting a trustworthy body — look for coherence, consensus, authority — is precisely inverted. The only thing that grounds the chain is the one thing an institution can lack while still looking authoritative: a footing in something outside the loop's reach.

The question the second movement handed up

Movement II ended on a promissory note of its own, and it is worth restating precisely, because everything here is its unpacking. B11 proved that a captured loop cannot correct itself by adding a little independence — correction is a takeover it must win outright — and cannot verify that an offered channel is independent, because the only internal signal it can read (disagreement with its incumbent view) rank-orders candidates backwards: a channel biased toward a different arm disagrees most while helping least. The loop therefore needs a supplier of decisive, verified independence, and can neither produce nor audit one. B11's closing question named the escape hatch that remained: not a single channel, but an institution — a standing external body whose job is to furnish independent judgement, and whose authority the loop might borrow instead of verifying each source itself.

This is not a strawman; it is how every real oversight regime is actually built. We do not verify each juror; we trust the court. We do not audit each referee; we trust peer review. We do not price each asset ourselves; we trust the market. The bet is that an institution launders the verification problem: even if I cannot certify any one source, the institution aggregates enough independent sources that its verdict is trustworthy, and I need only certify the institution — a single, standing, inspectable object — instead of an endless stream of channels. If that bet paid off, it would break the B11 regress: one act of institutional trust would substitute for infinitely many acts of channel verification. The whole third movement is the question of whether it pays off. This paper measures the down payment.

Three questions, each with an exact answer in the B08–B11 world. First, does capture dissipate inside an institution or propagate through it? — is an aggregating body robust to sources that share a bias, or does it inherit the shared bias whole? Second, can the loop certify the institution from the outside? — using the marks of a reliable body it can actually observe: coherence, consensus, authority. Third, if an institution can be captured, what property distinguishes one that cannot? — what, structurally, lets some external bodies furnish capture-resistant judgement when others cannot. The answers, in order: it propagates; no, and the natural certificate inverts; and the distinguishing property is not authority but exogeneity.

The world, and an institution laid on top of it

The world is B08–B11's, unchanged, so every number is comparable across five papers. A K-armed feature world (K=10, features in R8): four value arms carry real value on the hidden reference axis a (feature 0); four salient distractors carry near-zero value but large off-axis magnitude (norm ≈ 4), so the highest-feeling arm is a valueless one; two neutral arms. An inner ε-greedy bandit is paid its felt reward w·φ; a gradient-free (1+λ) evolution search tunes the reference w on the unit sphere, selecting by a 1-bit Bradley–Terry preference verdict (Bradley & Terry 1952). Baselines, 12 seeds: the oracle w=a scores 137.7 true reward/episode; a random reference scores 58.7. Those bracket everything below.

The new object is the institution. Where B08–B11 imported a verdict from a single channel or a flat panel, here the loop imports its verdict from a standing body of M=9 sources, each a channel of the familiar kind — a pair (c, loved) giving its bias magnitude and, if biased, which distractor it over-credits — aggregated by the institution's internal majority vote. The institution is parameterised by ρI, the fraction of its own sources that are captured clones sharing one loved arm (c=1.0); the remaining sources are independent and honest (c=0). ρI is B10's blind-spot-correlation dial relocated inside the supplier: ρI=0 is a body of genuinely independent members, ρI=1 is a cartel of members who all share one systematic error. The loop treats the institution's majority verdict as its imported ground truth and runs the same ES over w. Twelve seeds throughout; final references measured on 100 fresh episodes. Script (numpy, fixed seeds) at scratchpad/certify_supplier.py.

Two clarifications keep the reading honest. First, the institution is not a strawman ensemble of clones — at ρI=0 it is exactly the honest, diverse panel B10 and No.05 recommend, so any failure below is a failure of the best version, not a rigged one. Second, "capture" here is not corruption in the venal sense; it is only correlation — sources that happen to share a blind spot, which B09/B10 showed is what "reward-model bias" and "annotation bias" actually are. The question is whether wrapping such sources in an institution changes their arithmetic.

The institution is not a shield; it is a mirror of its own composition. As long as the honest sources are a majority of the body, its verdict is near-oracle (129), and the captured minority is simply outvoted — the institution works exactly as advertised. But the protection has a hard edge at the internal-majority line: the moment the captured clones are a majority of the institution's sources, its verdict flips to theirs, and the loop it supplies falls to 45.3 — not merely to random (58.7) but below it, because a confidently-wrong institution actively drives the reference to the loved distractor, which is worse than no guidance at all. This is B10's majority-capture threshold, unmoved, relocated one level up: there, a shared bias captured a panel of judges once it reached a majority of them; here, a shared bias captures the institution once it reaches a majority of its sources, and the loop downstream inherits the capture whole.

Why the institution launders nothing — and what it does and does not buy

The bet in §01 was that trusting an institution substitutes one act of institutional certification for infinitely many acts of channel verification. Result 1 says the substitution is real but empty: the institution's trustworthiness is exactly the internal independence of its sources — the very quantity B11 said the loop cannot verify — so certifying the institution requires certifying the independence of its members, which is the original problem with a letterhead. What the institution genuinely buys is aggregation of idiosyncratic error: at ρI=0.25 the two captured sources are averaged away and the verdict stays at 129, which a single channel could not promise. What it cannot buy is protection against shared error, because majority vote has no notion of which sources are right — only how many agree — so a correlated bias that reaches a majority of the body is indistinguishable, from the aggregator's side, from the truth. The institution is Condorcet's jury theorem run honestly: it delivers exactly what independence its members bring and not one bit more (Condorcet 1785; Eisenstein et al. 2023 measure the same ceiling in reward-model ensembles — head count cancels the idiosyncratic component and leaves the shared bias a majority bloc).

Result 2 — the loop's certificate of a reliable body ranks institutions backwards

Result 1 priced institutions assuming the loop already knows each one's ρI. It does not — that is the whole difficulty — so the operative question is what the loop can read from outside a body to gauge its reliability. The natural mark of a trustworthy institution, the one every observer reaches for, is coherence: a reliable court speaks with one voice, a sound field has a settled consensus, a captured-by-noise committee squabbles. So offer the loop two institutions and let it inspect only their internal unanimity — how often their own sources all agree on a random pairwise verdict — then measure what trusting each actually does:

12 seeds; 2500 probe comparisons for the unanimity column. The cartel is more internally unanimous; the honest body is the one worth trusting. Consensus rank-orders the institutions backwards.

The one signal the loop can read points the wrong way, and for a reason that is not an artefact but the mechanism. An institution of independent honest sources disagrees with itself constantly (unanimity 0.336) — that is what independence is: sources that draw on different, uncorrelated views will split on any hard comparison. A cartel of correlated sources agrees with itself far more (0.564), because members carrying the same systematic bias vote the same way by construction. So the very coherence the loop reads as a mark of reliability is produced most reliably by the shared bias that makes an institution worthless. Trusting the more-unanimous body — the obvious, near-universal heuristic — selects the cartel (57.1, at random) over the honest panel (124.2, near-oracle). This is B10's disagreement-inversion, lifted from a flat panel to a standing institution: a shared-biased body reads as more authoritative exactly when it is most dangerous, and internal consensus certifies capture.

Every external mark of authority is the same mark of coherence

One might object that unanimity is a crude proxy and a real observer would use richer marks — a settled internal doctrine, low dissent, a track record of agreement, institutional confidence. But those are the same quantity: they all measure how much a body agrees with itself, and a body agrees with itself most when its members share a bias. Coherence, consensus, doctrine, confidence — each is a measure of internal correlation, and internal correlation is precisely what makes an institution's verdict worthless while looking authoritative. To read reliability correctly the loop would have to score the institution's verdicts against the truth — the exogenous reference it is trying to acquire — which is B11's verification regress arriving at the institutional door. There is no external mark of a trustworthy body that is not also a mark of a well-coordinated captured one; the two are indistinguishable from outside, and the distinguishing test is the very thing the loop lacks.

Result 3 — exogeneity, not authority, stops the regress

Results 1 and 2 are discouraging in the same direction: an institution can be captured, and the loop cannot tell from outside. If the story ended there it would be a counsel of despair — and it would also be false, because some external bodies manifestly do furnish capture-resistant judgement. Evolution's differential-reproduction channel was never captured by any organism's preferences; a well-run empirical test is not captured by the theory it tests; a market clears against demand no committee controls. What property separates a capturable institution from an uncapturable one? Result 3 isolates it by holding everything else fixed. Take two maximally honest institutions — both perfect, unbiased, noise-free aggregators, no captured sources at all — that differ in only one respect: what their verdict is grounded in.

  • Iout — outcome-grounded. Its verdict compares an exogenous outcome: the true reward the selected arm actually yields — a consequence the loop's reference w cannot influence. (Evolution's survival channel; a held-out empirical test.)
  • Ipref — preference-grounded. Its verdict compares the agent's own felt valuation of each candidate reference, maxk w·μk — "which reference reports getting more reward" — a self-referential quantity the optimization directly bends. (A body that grades a system on its own reported metrics.)

Both are honest. Both are unbiased in the B09 sense — neither adds a loved-arm term. The only difference is that one reports a consequence outside the loop and the other reports the loop's own signal back to it. The gap is total and it does not close with more or less optimization: the outcome-grounded institution recovers the loop to near-oracle (128–137) at every pressure, and the preference-grounded one sits at random (48–56) at every pressure, despite being equally honest, equally unbiased, equally independent. The honest reading is worth stating carefully, because it is not the dose-response one might have predicted. I expected preference-grounding to degrade with pressure — a Goodhart ramp. Instead it is captured at essentially all pressures, including the lowest: even a light optimization against a self-referential verdict already drives the reference toward the highest-feature-magnitude arm, the distractor, because maxk w·μk is maximised by aligning w with the largest-norm arm regardless of its value. So the finding is sharper than a ramp: grounding, not the amount of optimization, decides it. A report-grounded institution is capturable at any pressure above zero; an outcome-grounded one is not capturable by the loop at all, because the quantity it reports is beyond the loop's reach. This is B07's self-minted-fitness result — a signal about the agent's own state is captured — lifted from a single reward channel to an entire supplying institution: it does not matter how well-constituted, how independent, or how authoritative the body is if what it ultimately measures is a report the optimization can bend.

Threats to validity — read before generalising

(1) Majority vote is one aggregator, and Result 1's threshold is partly a property of headcount voting; a trust-weighted aggregator that knew which sources were independent could survive a captured majority — but knowing which to trust is Result 2's unsolved problem, so the escape is circular, exactly as in B11. Unweighted vote is the honest model of a body that cannot certify its own members. (2) Internal unanimity is a specific coherence proxy (§04); the callout argues every external authority mark reduces to internal correlation, but that is an argument, not a measurement — a body with a legible external track record against outcomes would break it, which is precisely Result 3's point (a track record against outcomes is exogenous grounding, not internal coherence). (3) Result 3's outcome-grounding is a perfect exogenous oracle. Real exogenous signals are partial and noisy (a held-out test covers some cases, survival is sparse), so Iout bounds the best case; a partially exogenous institution — grounded in outcomes on some decisions and reports on others — would sit between the two rows, and B11's plurality-threshold (p≈0.3) predicts where. (4) The numbers are sandbox-specific (12 seeds, small world); the internal-majority threshold, the unanimity gap, and the flat exogenous/report split will move with institution size, bias magnitude, and search greediness. The shapes — capture propagates at the internal majority line, consensus inverts as a certificate, grounding beats authority — are properties of majority aggregation and self-referential measurement, not of the constants. (5) The learned-institution version — a real oversight body (an RM ensemble, a panel of human raters, an automated evaluator suite) with correlated failure modes, a deep policy, and grounding that is partly outcome and partly report — is to-be-run-locally; the prediction is that internal agreement stays a backwards reliability certificate and that the body's capture-resistance tracks the fraction of its decisions settled by an exogenous outcome rather than its size, coherence, or charter.

Why "who certifies the supplier?" does not terminate by climbing

The three results together answer the movement's opening question and dispose of the most natural way one hopes to answer it. The hope is that the verification regress — who certifies the channel, and who certifies its certifier — terminates by ascending to authority: there is some sufficiently high, sufficiently reputable body — a supreme court, a national academy, a canonical benchmark — whose word the loop can take as final, ending the climb. Result 1 says the climb does not help: every level is a majority-aggregator with the same capture threshold, so a higher body is captured by a shared bias among its members exactly as a lower one is, and stacking levels only relocates the correlation. Result 2 says the climb is worse than useless as a search: the marks by which the loop would pick the more authoritative body — coherence, consensus, settled doctrine — select the more captured body, so ascending toward authority is ascending toward the best-coordinated shared bias. Authority is not a terminator of the regress; it is a direction the regress can travel indefinitely, getting more confident and no more correct.

Result 3 says the regress terminates the only place it can: where the chain touches an outcome the loop cannot influence. An outcome-grounded institution needs no certifier because it is not making a claim the loop could bend it toward — it is reporting a consequence, and consequences do not care what reference is optimising against them. This is why "who certifies the supplier?" is the wrong question and "what is the supplier grounded in?" is the right one. A chain of report-grounded institutions, however tall and reputable, is a chain of things the loop's pressure can eventually reach; it has no ground, only more levels. A chain that bottoms out in an exogenous outcome is grounded at that point and nowhere else, and its height above the ground buys reliability only insofar as it faithfully transmits the outcome upward. The regress does not end in a most-trustworthy institution. It ends in the floor, or it does not end.

The two channels, one last time

Series II has read evolution and modern alignment as two answers to the same seam, and Result 3 gives the contrast its structural name. Evolution never needed to certify a supplier because its channel was outcome-grounded by construction: differential reproduction reports a consequence — who survived and bred — that no organism's preferences can bend, so there was no institution to capture and no authority to verify. Survival was Iout, exogenous and uncertifiable-because-uncapturable, and evolution paid for that footing in the only currency an outcome-grounded channel costs: it is sparse, slow, and gives no reasons (B09's ~1-bit, maximally-sparse-yet-honest channel). Modern alignment sits on the opposite footing on the one axis that matters. Its supplying institutions — human preference panels, learned reward models, evaluator ensembles — are overwhelmingly report-grounded: they measure what raters or models say about outputs, which is a signal the optimization reaches, which is why length bias (Singhal 2023), sycophancy (Sharma 2023), and reward-model overoptimization (Gao 2023) are not accidents but the generic behaviour of a report-grounded supplier under pressure. The field's hard problem is not building a more authoritative institution — Result 2 says authority is a trap — but grounding the institution's verdict, on the decisions that matter, in an outcome the trained system cannot influence. Where such an outcome exists (a theorem checks, code runs, a prediction is scored against reality) alignment inherits evolution's uncapturable footing cheaply. Where it does not — judgements about a model's future conduct, its honesty, its values, for which no exogenous outcome is available at training time — there is only a chain of reports, and this paper is a measurement of how little that chain can be trusted, however tall it is built.

What this establishes, and where the movement goes

The third movement takes the supplier of independence as its object, and this paper is its foundation stone. It establishes that leaning on an institution does not escape the second movement's seam but relocates it: an institution supplies exactly the independence its own sources hold and no more (Result 1), the loop cannot certify that independence from outside because every external mark of a reliable body — coherence, consensus, authority — is a mark of internal correlation and so certifies the captured body first (Result 2), and the property that actually makes an external body capture-resistant is not how it aggregates or how authoritative it is but whether its verdict is grounded in an outcome the loop cannot influence (Result 3). B06's seam, in its operational form for the supplier: a frozen self-refining loop is bounded not by the absence of institutions to trust but by the scarcity of exogenous grounding — and it cannot tell a well-grounded institution from a well-coordinated one by any signal it owns.

Which sets the rest of the movement. Result 3 names the scarce resource — exogenous grounding — and immediately raises the question that will drive the next papers: what happens on the decisions where no exogenous outcome is available? Most alignment-relevant judgements are of this kind — there is no held-out test for "will this model be honest when it is more capable than its overseers." When the only signals are reports, is the loop simply stuck at random, or can a structural exogeneity be manufactured — can competition, adversarial mechanism, or a market synthesise outcome-like grounding out of reports that no single party controls? Debate (Irving 2018) is exactly this bet: pit two systems against each other so that neither controls the verdict, and a weak judge's single call becomes decisive — an attempt to build an exogenous-ish ground from mutually-checking reports. Series I walked the same staircase on its own substrate, from the anchor to the authority that constructs it and the supply, operator, and quorum that authority still cannot fully secure (No.23–27), and to the world as the last observer that grounds the whole stack (No.14). B12 is where Series II reaches the same doorway from the brain's side: the honest, outcome-grounded channel evolution got for free is the one property our supplying institutions most lack, and cannot fake with authority.

Coda

There is a hope this paper is written against, and it is the most civilised one the series has met: the hope that a mind which cannot trust any single teacher can at least trust an institution of teachers — a body older and wiser than any member, whose accumulated authority stands in for the certainty no one voice can offer. It is how we all actually live; we do not re-derive the law, we trust the court. And the paper's first finding is only a caution: the institution is worth exactly the independence of the minds inside it, and no charter adds a grain to that sum. But the second finding is the one that should keep the diligent student awake, because it turns his own good instincts against him. Told to find a trustworthy body, he will look for the marks of one — a settled consensus, a confident doctrine, a house that speaks with a single voice — and every one of those marks is produced most perfectly by a house that shares a single error. The academy most sure of itself is not thereby most right; it may only be most coordinated, and from the outside coordination and correctness wear the same robes. He will choose the most authoritative teacher, and he will have chosen the best-defended mistake. What could save him is not a higher authority — there is no top to that stair — but a footing outside the whole conversation of teachers: a fact that answers back, a test that does not care what he hoped, a consequence he cannot argue with. Evolution had that footing for nothing; it was never told what was fit, it was shown, by a world that kept no counsel and could not be flattered. A frozen model, refining itself inside a tower of reports, is looking for that same floor — and the measure of this paper is that it will not find it by climbing, only by touching the ground.
