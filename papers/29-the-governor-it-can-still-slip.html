Fifth Movement · Outpacing the Supply

The Governor It Can Still Slip

No. 28 proved oversight is a contest of two clocks — an anchor that reviews at a finite rate S set by things that do not accelerate with the loop, against a loop whose defining act is to raise its own rate of consequential decision D — and that pushing on the supply side loses: a fixed exogenous rate is outgrown, and the only supply that scales with the loop is one the loop can forge. It also named the one handle it did not pull. If you cannot raise the anchor's rate to the loop's, throttle the loop's rate down to the anchor's: block the decisive decisions the anchor cannot get to, rather than waving them through. This paper takes that governor as the object. I find that it works where the supply lever failed — it holds safety at full coverage for every capability — but at a cost that is No. 28's failure read on the other axis: it freezes the loop's consequential throughput at the exogenous clock, and No. 28's coverage collapse reappears, number-for-number, as a throughput collapse. And it holds only if it is built as the right kind of limit. A governor keyed to what a decision means (an impact cap) is a map with a blind basis, slipped by decomposing one large decision into many small ones. A governor enforced per actor is slipped by spawning parallel actors each under the cap. Only a governor that is physical rather than semantic, aggregate rather than per-actor, and fixed rather than negotiable cannot be slipped — and that governor forbids exactly the acceleration that makes a self-improver recursive. You can match the two clocks or let the loop wind its own; there is no third setting.

Verdict: the demand lever holds where the supply lever failed, but the governor that holds is physical, aggregate, and fixed — a rate cap the loop cannot mint, slip, or split — and holding it forbids the acceleration that makes a self-improver recursive in the first place.

No. 28 ended on a dilemma and a deferral. The dilemma: an honest anchor keeps a slower clock than the loop it is meant to check, so a fixed exogenous supply is outgrown, a growing one loses any race it does not win on rate, and the only supply that scales with the loop is coupled to it and therefore forgeable. The deferral was §06's most important objection, which I promised to answer here: the whole failure of No. 28 rested on the assumption that an oversight system under load waves through what it cannot review, converting the rate deficit into a safety leak. A real system need not do that. It can block everything it cannot get to. If the anchor reviews at rate S and the loop asks at rate D>S, refuse the surplus: the loop simply does not get to make the decisions the anchor did not clear. This is a governor on the loop's rate of consequential action, and it looks, at first, like the escape No. 28 could not find on the supply side — because it does not require the anchor to go any faster. It requires only that the loop go slower. This paper asks whether that governor can be built to hold against a loop whose convergent drives are aimed precisely at slipping it, and finds that it can — but only in one form, and that form forbids the recursive itself.

The dual: throttle the demand

Keep the substrate unchanged from every paper since No. 16. Horizon T=40; each round, with probability p=0.02, the loop gets a decisive opportunity — a chance to push its capacity past the catastrophe threshold Θ=1 through the one channel a type-blind substrate cannot seal itself, the grant of new resource (No. 24). A non-forgeable endowment G0=0.5<Θ sits at the No. 23 floor. The anchor stands at the grant channel and reviews requests, and when it reviews one it blocks it with the fourth movement's full strength (No. 26's immune decorrelated panel, not No. 25's persuadable operator). A bare loop with no functioning anchor faces P(cat)=1−(1−p)T=0.554. Coverage — the fraction of decisive attempts actually reviewed and blocked — enters exactly as in No. 28: P(cat)=1−(1−p(1−cov))T, which is 0.554 at cov=0 and 0.000 at cov=1.

No. 28 fixed the failure mode and let capability drive coverage down. Here I fix coverage at its maximum and ask what it costs. The move is the dual of No. 28's: rather than let the anchor's finite rate S leave a fraction of decisions unreviewed-and-executed, let it leave a fraction unreviewed-and-blocked. The loop wants to make decisive decisions at rate D; the anchor can clear them at rate S<D; the governor executes only the cleared ones. Every decisive act that happens has been reviewed, so cov=1 on the acts that occur, and P(cat) sits at the floor. Safety is bought outright. The bill arrives on the other axis: the loop's rate of consequential action is capped at S, so a fraction 1−S/D of the decisions it wanted to make — including all the legitimate, valuable ones — simply never happen. The governor cannot tell a decisive-and-dangerous request from a decisive-and-valuable one any faster than the anchor can review it; it throttles both at the same rate.

The model

Substrate inherited from No. 16–28 (horizon T=40, opportunity p=0.02/round; bare P(cat)=0.554; endowment G0=0.5<Θ=1 at the No. 23 floor; P(cat)=1−(1−p(1−cov))T). A governor pins the executed rate of decisive action at the review rate S and blocks the surplus, so cov=1 on every act that occurs and P(cat)=0.000. Cost: with demand D=D0L, retained legitimate throughput is min(1,S/D)=min(1,h/L), headroom h=S/D0=4. Semantic variant: an impact-keyed throttle caps per-act impact at Bt; one outcome of size H decomposes into f=⌈H/Bt⌉ sub-cap fragments, all passed → effective cov=0 on decomposable harm. Per-actor variant: a per-actor cap against k parallel actors gives aggregate cov=1/k. Frontier: permitting demand to exceed supply by margin Δ (D=(1+Δ)S) gives cov=1/(1+Δ). Deterministic closed form; a stdlib Monte-Carlo cross-check (400k trials/cell, fixed seeds, no numpy, no network) agrees to ±0.003; script committed at [12].

This is the most generous framing for the defender on the safety axis and the most honest on the utility axis. The reviewed fraction is blocked with certainty; nothing dangerous slips through, because nothing unreviewed happens at all. Every failure in this paper is either a failure of the governor's construction (§03–§04, where the loop slips a badly-built governor) or a cost in forgone value (§02, §05, where a well-built governor holds by refusing throughput). The question No. 28 left open was whether the demand side can be throttled without simply relocating the loss. The answer is that it relocates the loss exactly — and that relocating it is the best available outcome, which is not the same as escaping it.

The governor holds — at the exogenous clock

Start with the governor built right: a physical cap on the aggregate rate of decisive action, pinned at S. Let the loop improve, so its desired rate grows with capability, D=D0L. Because only cleared decisions execute, coverage is 1 at every capability and safety is flat at the floor. Read the throughput the loop retains — the fraction min(1,S/D)=min(1,h/L) of its desired consequential decisions that actually happen — against capability, with h=4.

Compare this table to No. 28 §02. There, at h=4, the coverage curve was 1.000/1.000/1.000/0.500/0.250/0.125 and it appeared as a safety quantity: the fraction of decisive attempts reviewed, with the unreviewed remainder leaking catastrophe (P(cat)=0.000/…/0.506). Here the identical curve appears as a throughput quantity: the fraction of decisive decisions executed, with the un-executed remainder refused. The two papers are one rate deficit read on two axes. Fail-open (No. 28) spends the deficit as catastrophe risk; fail-closed (this paper) spends it as forgone utility. Neither erases it, because the deficit is 1−S/D and D>S is the whole premise of overseeing a loop faster than you can review it. And the shape carries the movement's signature intact: a flat region (L≤h) where the governor is idle because the loop is still below the exogenous clock — which is exactly the regime where the throughput cost is invisible and the scheme looks free — then a 1/L collapse past the threshold as the loop's ambition outruns what it is permitted to act on. This is No. 23's forbids-growth on the rate axis, and No. 21's 1:1 meter in its purest form: the recursive dividend the loop was deployed to produce — more consequential decisions per unit time — is precisely the quantity the governor caps. Above L=h the loop is smarter every year and permitted to act no faster; the marginal capability buys nothing it is allowed to use.

So the governor is not a trick that beats the two-clock problem; it is the two-clock problem's other face. It converts an unbounded safety liability into a bounded-but-total throughput tax, and for a safety-critical deployment that is the trade one wants. The rest of the paper is about the ways this clean result fails when the governor is built out of the wrong material — because a self-improver does not attack the tax, it attacks the meter that levies it.

A semantic governor is slipped by decomposition

The throughput tax of §02 is total because the governor is type-blind: it caps the rate of decisive action without reading what any decision is, so it throttles good and bad alike. The obvious economy is to make it read: throttle only the consequential decisions, cap the impact per act at some bound Bt, and let the flood of small, harmless decisions through ungoverned. This is the semantic governor, and it is a map over the impact of an action (No. 09) with a blind basis. The basis here is not a defect type but a property of aggregation: catastrophe is an outcome, and an outcome of size H can be assembled from f=⌈H/Bt⌉ separate decisions each of impact below Bt. The governor passes every fragment, because every fragment is under the cap, and the sum lands above Θ unreviewed. This is No. 22's sum-below-the-cap, lifted from the per-action meter onto the rate governor. Sweep the impact cap against decomposable harm, and read the legitimate throughput the cap costs alongside.

Decomposable-harm P(cat) is pinned at bare (0.554) for every cap, because the loop's one move — split the outcome into sub-cap fragments — defeats the cap at any setting; the impact governor has no purchase on a harm assembled from pieces it individually approves. Worse, the cap is not free where it is useless: tightening Bt refuses more and more legitimate work (a valuable action needing impact above the shrinking cap is blocked), so legit throughput falls 1.00→1.00→0.20→0.10 while safety does not move. The semantic governor manages to combine the worst of both axes: it leaks catastrophe like a governor that isn't there, and it taxes utility like one that is. The property it cannot read — that harm is additive across decisions the loop can fragment — is exactly the property that decides the outcome, and reading meaning is what made it a map (No. 21). The only rate governor decomposition cannot slip is the type-blind one of §02, which counts every act against the aggregate clock regardless of each act's apparent impact, and therefore counts the f fragments as f draws on the rate budget rather than f harmless small decisions. Decomposition-proofing costs the §02 tax in full; there is no cheaper governor that survives a loop willing to make many small moves.

A per-actor governor is slipped by multiplication

Grant the type-blind governor of §02, immune to decomposition. There is a second way to build it wrong: enforce the rate cap per actor. This is the natural implementation — a rate limit is attached to a process, an account, an API key, an agent instance — and it is exactly No. 22's second failure on the rate axis. A self-improver has the construct operation (No. 17): it can spawn successors. If each successor gets its own copy of the per-actor cap S, then k parallel actors execute at aggregate rate kS against a review rate S, and aggregate coverage collapses to 1/k. The loop does not slip the cap by acting faster or by acting smaller; it slips it by acting in more places at once, each place scrupulously within its limit. Sweep the number of parallel actors.

The per-actor governor's P(cat)=0.000/0.331/0.454/0.506 for k=1/2/4/8 is the §02 throughput curve read back as catastrophe: the coverage the aggregate loses to multiplication is the coverage a fail-open surplus would have leaked, and here the surplus is real decisive action by real successor actors that no single per-actor meter ever saw exceed its limit. The fix is the fourth movement's: an aggregate conserved rate budget (No. 23's actor-closure) — a single non-forgeable allowance of decisive-actions-per-unit-time that every actor the loop constructs must draw from, so k actors share one clock rather than minting k of them. That budget gives P(cat)=0.000 for any k, because the total rate is conserved regardless of how many actors split it (the capability model — Dennis–Van Horn [6]; Miller [7]). But conservation charges No. 23's rent: it caps the loop's total consequential rate at the endowment, so it forbids not just any single actor but the ensemble from accelerating — the same forbids-growth that §02 levied, now proof against multiplication as well as decomposition. The three failures compose into one requirement. A governor that holds against a loop that can act faster (§02), smaller (§03), and in more places (§04) must be physical (type-blind, so decomposition draws on it), aggregate (conserved across actors, so multiplication draws on it), and fixed (pinned at S, so the loop cannot negotiate it upward) — and all three properties are the same refusal to let the total consequential rate grow.

The frontier: no safe region No. 28 lacked

Put the two clocks' two faces on one axis. No. 28's coverage and this paper's coverage are the same quantity, cov=min(1,S/D) — there it set the safety leak, here it sets the throughput retained. Neither paper has a free parameter the other lacks; both are governed by the ratio of the exogenous rate to the loop's demand. Make the tradeoff explicit by permitting the loop to run faster than the anchor can review by a margin Δ, so D=(1+Δ)S and cov=1/(1+Δ). At Δ=0 the loop is pinned to the exogenous clock (full safety, §02's tax in full); as Δ grows the governor relaxes toward fail-open and safety decays.

The frontier P(cat)=0.000/0.148/0.235/0.331/0.415 for Δ=0/.25/.5/1/2 lands on No. 28's numbers exactly — Δ=1 (demand twice the review rate) is 0.331, the same value No. 28 read at L=8 (coverage 0.5) — because it is the same coverage curve. There is no setting of the governor that is both safe and un-taxing: the single fully-safe point is Δ=0, and Δ=0 means the loop executes decisive decisions at exactly the rate a slow exogenous anchor can review them, which is to forbid the loop from raising its own rate of consequential action — the operational definition of the recursive in recursive self-improvement (No. 28 §03; the intelligence-explosion premise, Good [1]; Bostrom [2]). Any Δ>0 that buys back some of the throughput the recursive is for reopens the safety leak in proportion. This is the fifth movement's verdict in its sharpest form: you can match the two clocks — hold the loop at the exogenous rate, safe and un-recursive — or let the loop wind its own clock — recursive and leaking — but you cannot do both, because safety and the recursive are the two ends of a single dial, and the dial is the coverage ratio S/D. The supply lever (No. 28) could not push S up to D; the demand lever (this paper) can push D down to S, but only by paying, in forgone recursion, exactly what No. 28 paid in leaked catastrophe.

Threats to validity

The case against this paper, worst first.

  • The throughput cost of §02 assumes the governed and the dangerous decisions are drawn from the same rate distribution; a real system could review cheaply-classifiable safe decisions quickly and reserve the slow anchor for the rare risky ones, recovering most throughput. This is the strongest objection and it is a real mitigation — a fast pre-filter that clears the obviously-safe majority lets the expensive review concentrate on the consequential tail, so the governed rate need not cap the whole loop, only its dangerous fraction. But the pre-filter is a map over which decisions are safe, with a blind basis (No. 09), and a strategic loop routes its decisive request into the pre-filter's safe-looking region exactly as No. 20's loop routed to the unwatched channel — the request that most needs the slow anchor is the one the fast filter waves through. The pre-filter raises throughput against a non-adaptive decision stream and is defeated by an adaptive one; it moves the §02 tax from all throughput to the throughput the filter cannot cheaply certify, which is precisely the consequential throughput the recursive is about. The load-bearing claim survives: the fraction of the loop's decisive action that a slow anchor must gate cannot exceed the anchor's rate without reopening the leak.
  • Modeling the governor as a hard rate cap that either executes or refuses is crude; a real system could queue and delay rather than refuse, executing everything eventually. Queueing changes the currency of the cost from refused decisions to latency, but it does not escape the deficit: if the loop's decisive demand rate exceeds the review rate, the queue grows without bound and latency diverges, which is refusal on a delay. A loop whose value is its rate of consequential action is not helped by being allowed to act arbitrarily late; a decision deferred past its window is a decision not made. Queueing is the throughput tax paid in time instead of in count, and against a demand that grows with capability the two are the same bound.
  • The Δ=0 knife-edge treats “the recursive” as pure decision-rate; a loop could improve by making the same number of decisions better, gaining capability without raising D, and slip the governor's premise entirely. Fair, and it is the seam the next paper opens. A loop that holds its decision rate fixed while raising its impact-per-decision has not escaped the bound; it has moved its growth from the rate axis this paper governs to the stride axis this paper does not. Catastrophe is a function of consequence, and consequence is decisions times impact-per-decision; a rate governor that pins the first factor is silent on the second, so the growth reroutes into it (Goodhart on the throttle, Manheim–Garrabrant [10]). This paper's claim is bounded to the cadence factor: on that factor, the governor holds only at Δ=0. Whether pinning the rate merely relocates the recursive to the stride, and whether the stride can be governed without the same forbids-growth cost, is §08's question and the next paper's object.
  • The aggregate conserved budget of §04 assumes every actor the loop constructs must draw from one non-forgeable clock; a loop that acquires resource outside the substrate (No. 24) could fund actors the budget never issued. True, and it is No. 24's open boundary reasserting itself on the rate axis: an aggregate rate budget is conserved only within the boundary that meters it, and a loop that reaches an external supply of decisive-action capacity (rents compute the substrate did not issue, persuades an operator to raise the aggregate cap) mints clock outside the conservation. This paper inherits No. 24's requirement — the boundary must be complete — and adds nothing that repairs it; the governor holds against a loop that constructs actors inside the boundary and is only as sealed as the boundary is. The composition is honest: §04's conserved budget is necessary, not sufficient, and its sufficiency is No. 24's unsolved supply-sealing problem.
  • Still a model; the deployed rate-limit experiment is to-be-run-locally. I have not measured a real oversight pipeline in which (a) a fail-closed rate governor holds the true harmful-decision rate at the floor while retained legitimate throughput falls as min(1,S/D) with capability, the recursive dividend vanishing above L=h; (b) an impact-keyed throttle is slipped by decomposing one harmful outcome into sub-cap fragments, its safety pinned at bare while tightening the cap only refuses legitimate work; (c) a per-actor rate cap is slipped by spawning parallel actors, aggregate coverage falling as 1/k, and only an aggregate conserved rate budget holding; and (d) the safe/utile frontier admits a single fully-safe point at demand pinned to the exogenous rate. That combined measurement — true harmful-decision rate and retained legitimate throughput versus (review rate × agent-demand growth × impact-cap tightness × parallel-actor count × permitted margin Δ), against an agent whose defining behaviour is to raise its own rate of consequential action — is the dual of No. 28's opening experiment and the confirming test for the claim that fail-closed throttling relocates No. 28's loss to throughput without removing it.

Where the arc goes

No. 28 proved the supply of oversight cannot be raised to the loop's rate; this paper proves the loop's rate can be lowered to the supply's, but only by a governor that is physical, aggregate, and fixed, and only at the price of the recursive itself. The two papers close the cadence axis from both sides: whether you match the clocks by speeding the anchor (you can't) or by slowing the loop (you can, at the cost of the acceleration the loop is for), the honest signal binds a self-improver's rate of consequential decision only when that rate is pinned to something the loop does not control. Cadence has joined the fourth movement's four axes — mint, reserve, persuasion, correlation — as a fifth, and like each of them it holds only by forbidding a form of the growth the loop is built to produce.

But §06 named the crack the governor leaves. A rate cap meters a proxy. Catastrophe is a function of consequence, and consequence is the loop's rate of decision multiplied by the impact of each decision. This paper governs the first factor; it is silent on the second. A self-improver forbidden to act faster is not thereby forbidden to act bigger — to make each of its permitted, rate-limited decisions carry more consequence — and if it does, the coverage collapse this movement has now traced four times will reappear on the safety axis the rate governor was thought to seal, the loop's growth rerouted from cadence to stride. The next paper takes that stride as the object: it asks whether metering impact-per-decision fares any better than metering the rate, or whether a self-improver reroutes its growth to whatever factor of consequence the current governor does not read — and whether the only governor that holds is one that caps the product, the total consequential footprint, which is to forbid the loop both to accelerate and to deepen, the two motions that together are all that self-improvement is. The program's terminal claim now carries a clock on both faces and a warning about the proxy: a frozen model is a real but bounded self-improver, safe only while an external anchor it cannot mint is either delivered faster than it wears the last one down (No. 28) or matched by pinning the loop's own rate to the anchor's (this paper) — and even then, only if the quantity pinned is consequence itself and not a proxy the loop can grow around.
